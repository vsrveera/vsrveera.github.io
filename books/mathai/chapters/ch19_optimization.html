19  Optimization

NoteKey ideas
  • An optimiser turns a gradient into a parameter update. The simplest rule is \(\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\,g\).
  • SGD uses the gradient of a random minibatch. It is an unbiased estimate with variance that falls as \(1/B\).
  • Momentum averages past gradients. It speeds up along valleys and damps zig-zag.
  • Adaptive methods (RMSProp, Adam) divide each coordinate’s step by its recent gradient size.
  • Learning-rate schedules (warmup, cosine decay) matter as much as the choice of optimiser.

Chapter 18 gives us \(\nabla_\theta L\) for the cost of a few forward passes. Chapter 17 showed that the gradient gives the direction of steepest descent but says nothing about how far to go, or what to do when the surface is a long narrow valley.

Given a gradient, how large and in what direction should each parameter step be?

19.2 Gradient descent

The basic update (Chapter 1) is

\[ \theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta\,\nabla L(\theta_t). \tag{19.1}\]

We test it on an ill-conditioned quadratic: \(f(x,y)=\frac12(x^2+50\,y^2)\). The curvature is 1 along \(x\) and 50 along \(y\). The condition number is \(\kappa=50\).

On a quadratic, each coordinate decouples: \(y_{t+1}=(1-50\eta)\,y_t\) and \(x_{t+1}=(1-\eta)\,x_t\). We need \(|1-50\eta|<1\) for stability. This is the condition \(\eta<2/\lambda_{\max}=0.04\) (derived in Chapter 20).

grad = lambda p: np.array([p[0], 50 * p[1]])
f = lambda p: 0.5 * (p[0] ** 2 + 50 * p[1] ** 2)

def run_gd(lr, steps, p0=(8.0, 1.0)):
    p = np.array(p0); path = [p.copy()]
    for _ in range(steps):
        p = p - lr * grad(p); path.append(p.copy())
    return np.array(path)

gx, gy = np.meshgrid(np.linspace(-9, 9, 200), np.linspace(-1.6, 1.6, 200))
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 3.4))
for ax, lr, n in zip(axes, [0.036, 0.01], [40, 40]):
    P = run_gd(lr, n)
    ax.contour(gx, gy, 0.5 * (gx ** 2 + 50 * gy ** 2), levels=12, cmap="viridis")

19.3 Stochastic gradient descent

The loss is an average over \(N\) examples: \(L(\theta)=\frac1N\sum_{i}\ell_i(\theta)\). The full gradient needs all \(N\). For large \(N\) this is too slow for every step.

SGD picks a random minibatch \(\mathcal{B}\) of \(B\) examples and uses

\[ \hat{g}=\frac1B\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\nabla\ell_i(\theta). \tag{19.2}\]

TipWhy the minibatch gradient is a good estimate

Draw each example uniformly at random. Then each \(\nabla\ell_i\) has expectation \(\nabla L\). By linearity of expectation (Chapter 15), \[ \mathbb{E}[\hat{g}]=\frac1B\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\mathbb{E}[\nabla\ell_i]=\nabla L. \] The estimate is unbiased. For independent draws with per-example gradient variance \(\sigma^2\), the variance of the average is \(\sigma^2/B\). The error shrinks like \(1/\sqrt{B}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Four times as many examples per step halves the noise, but costs four times as much compute. We verify both properties on the line-fit loss.

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
N = 5000
x = rng.uniform(-2, 2, N); y = 2 * x + 1 + rng.normal(0, 0.5, N)
a, b = 0.5, 0.0
per_ex = 2 * (a * x + b - y) * x                          # ∂ℓ_i/∂a for every example
full = per_ex.mean()

Bs = [1, 4, 16, 64, 256, 1024]
stds, means = [], []
for B in Bs:
    g = np.array([per_ex[rng.integers(0, N, B)].mean() for _ in range(2000)])
    stds.append(g.std()); means.append(g.mean())
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(5.6, 3.4))
ax.loglog(Bs, stds, "o-", color=PALETTE["loss"], label="measured")
ax.loglog(Bs, stds[0] / np.sqrt(Bs), "--", color=PALETTE["neutral"], label="∝ 1/√B")
ax.set_xlabel("batch size B"); ax.set_ylabel("std of minibatch gradient"); ax.legend()
plt.show()

full gradient -4.010;  mean of batch gradients [-3.869 -4.077 -3.994 -3.991 -4.017 -4.014]

Noise has benefits and costs. It lets the optimiser jitter out of shallow traps (Chapter 20). It also prevents settling exactly at a minimum unless the learning rate is lowered.

19.4 Momentum

Along a valley floor, successive gradients mostly agree. Across it, they alternate. Momentum keeps a running sum of past gradients and steps along it:

\[ \mathbf{v}_{t+1}=\beta\,\mathbf{v}_t+\mathbf{g}_t,\qquad \theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta\,\mathbf{v}_{t+1}. \tag{19.3}\]

Unrolling, \(\mathbf{v}_t=\sum_{i}\beta^{t-i}\mathbf{g}_i\): an exponentially weighted sum of past gradients. If the gradient is constant, \(\mathbf{v}\to\mathbf{g}/(1-\beta)\). With \(\beta=0.9\) the effective step is 10 times larger along consistent directions. Components that flip sign cancel.

19.5 RMSProp and Adam

Momentum fixes direction. It does not fix the scale mismatch between parameters. In a network, gradients of different layers can differ by orders of magnitude.

RMSProp keeps a running average of the squared gradient per parameter and divides by its square root:

\[ \mathbf{s}_{t+1}=\rho\,\mathbf{s}_t+(1-\rho)\,\mathbf{g}_t^{2},\qquad \theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta\,\frac{\mathbf{g}_t}{\sqrt{\mathbf{s}_{t+1}}+\epsilon}. \tag{19.4}\]

Each coordinate takes a step of roughly size \(\eta\), whatever the scale of its gradient.

Adam combines momentum (first moment \(\mathbf{m}\)) with RMSProp (second moment \(\mathbf{v}\)):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{m}_t&=\beta_{1}\mathbf{m}_{t-1}+(1-\beta_1)\mathbf{g}_t,& \hat{\mathbf{m}}_t&=\mathbf{m}_t/(1-\beta_1^t),\\ \mathbf{v}_t&=\beta_{2}\mathbf{v}_{t-1}+(1-\beta_2)\mathbf{g}_t^2,& \hat{\mathbf{v}}_t&=\mathbf{v}_t/(1-\beta_2^t),\\ \theta_t&=\theta_{t-1}-\eta\,\frac{\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t}{\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t}+\epsilon}. \end{aligned} \tag{19.5}\]

The common values are \(\beta_1=0.9\), \(\beta_2=0.999\), \(\epsilon=10^{-8}\).

19.5.1 Why the bias correction

The averages start at \(\mathbf{m}_0=\mathbf{0}\). Early on they are too small, because most of the weight sits on the zero.

TipDerivation: the factor \(1-\beta^t\)

Unrolling, \(\mathbf{m}_t=(1-\beta_1)\sum_{i=1}^{t}\beta_1^{\,t-i}\mathbf{g}_i\). If the gradients have a common mean \(\bar{\mathbf{g}}\), \[ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{m}_t]=(1-\beta_1)\,\bar{\mathbf{g}}\sum_{i=1}^{t}\beta_1^{\,t-i}=(1-\beta_1)\,\bar{\mathbf{g}}\,\frac{1-\beta_1^t}{1-\beta_1}=(1-\beta_1^t)\,\bar{\mathbf{g}}. \] So \(\mathbf{m}_t\) underestimates \(\bar{\mathbf{g}}\) by the factor \(1-\beta_1^t\). Dividing by it removes the bias. The same argument applies to \(\mathbf{v}_t\). \(\blacksquare\)

19.6 Learning-rate schedules

The learning rate need not be constant. Common schedules:

  • Warmup: increase \(\eta\) linearly from 0 over the first steps. Early gradient statistics are noisy, and a large first step can break a model.
  • Cosine decay: \(\eta_t=\eta_{\min}+\frac12(\eta_{\max}-\eta_{\min})\bigl(1+\cos\frac{\pi t}{T}\bigr)\). Large steps early, small ones late, for fine convergence.
T, warm = 1000, 100
t = np.arange(T)
const = np.full(T, 1.0)
step = 1.0 * 0.3 ** (t // 300)
cosine = 0.5 * (1 + np.cos(np.pi * t / T))
warm_cos = np.where(t < warm, t / warm, 0.5 * (1 + np.cos(np.pi * (t - warm) / (T - warm))))
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.2, 3.4))
for y, name, c in [(const, "constant", PALETTE["neutral"]), (step, "step decay", PALETTE["data"]),
                   (cosine, "cosine", PALETTE["model"]), (warm_cos, "warmup + cosine", PALETTE["grad"])]:
    ax.plot(t, y, label=name, color=c, lw=2)
ax.set_xlabel("step"); ax.set_ylabel("η / η_max"); ax.legend(fontsize=9)

19.7 A race on the Rosenbrock valley

We implement the four optimisers in a few lines each. They take the parameter, the gradient, a state dictionary, and the step number.

def sgd(lr):
    return lambda p, g, s, t: p - lr * g

def momentum(lr, beta=0.9):
    def step(p, g, s, t):
        s["v"] = beta * s.get("v", 0.0) + g
        return p - lr * s["v"]
    return step

def rmsprop(lr, rho=0.9, eps=1e-8):
    def step(p, g, s, t):
        s["s"] = rho * s.get("s", 0.0) + (1 - rho) * g * g
        return p - lr * g / (np.sqrt(s["s"]) + eps)
    return step

def adam(lr, b1=0.9, b2=0.999, eps=1e-8):
    def step(p, g, s, t):
        s["m"] = b1 * s.get("m", 0.0) + (1 - b1) * g
        s["v"] = b2 * s.get("v", 0.0) + (1 - b2) * g * g
        m_hat = s["m"] / (1 - b1 ** t); v_hat = s["v"] / (1 - b2 ** t)
        return p - lr * m_hat / (np.sqrt(v_hat) + eps)
    return step

rosen = lambda p: (1 - p[0]) ** 2 + 100 * (p[1] - p[0] ** 2) ** 2
def rosen_grad(p):
    x, y = p
    return np.array([-2 * (1 - x) - 400 * x * (y - x ** 2), 200 * (y - x ** 2)])

def run(opt, steps, p0=(-1.5, 2.0)):
    p, state = np.array(p0), {}
    path, vals = [p.copy()], [rosen(p)]
    for t in range(1, steps + 1):
        p = opt(p, rosen_grad(p), state, t)
        path.append(p.copy()); vals.append(rosen(p))
    return np.array(path), np.array(vals)

19.8 Training a network

We train the digits MLP of Chapter 11 with three optimisers from the same start, for 300 full-batch steps.

import torch, torch.nn as nn
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
D = load_digits()
Xtr, Xte, ytr, yte = train_test_split(D.data / 16.0, D.target, test_size=0.25,
                                      random_state=0, stratify=D.target)
Xt, yt = torch.tensor(Xtr, dtype=torch.float32), torch.tensor(ytr)
Xv, yv = torch.tensor(Xte, dtype=torch.float32), torch.tensor(yte)

def train(make_opt, steps=300):
    torch.manual_seed(0)
    m = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(64, 32), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(32, 10))
    opt = make_opt(m.parameters()); hist = []
    for _ in range(steps):
        opt.zero_grad()
        loss = nn.functional.cross_entropy(m(Xt), yt)
        loss.backward(); opt.step(); hist.append(loss.item())
    acc = (m(Xv).argmax(1) == yv).float().mean().item()
    return hist, acc

runs = {"SGD (lr 0.5)":         lambda p: torch.optim.SGD(p, lr=0.5),
        "SGD+momentum (lr 0.1)": lambda p: torch.optim.SGD(p, lr=0.1, momentum=0.9),
        "Adam (lr 0.01)":        lambda p: torch.optim.Adam(p, lr=0.01)}
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.4, 3.5))
for (name, mk), c in zip(runs.items(), [PALETTE["neutral"], PALETTE["model"], PALETTE["loss"]]):
    hist, acc = train(mk)
    ax.semilogy(hist, label=name, color=c, lw=2)
    print(f"{name:24s} final loss {hist[-1]:.4f}   test accuracy {acc:.3f}")
ax.set_xlabel("step"); ax.set_ylabel("training loss"); ax.legend()
plt.show()

19.9 Production: checking against PyTorch

Our optimisers should do exactly what torch.optim does. We run both for 100 steps on Rosenbrock and compare the paths.

def torch_path(make_opt, steps=100, p0=(-1.5, 2.0)):
    p = torch.tensor(p0, dtype=torch.float64, requires_grad=True)
    opt = make_opt([p]); out = []
    for _ in range(steps):
        opt.zero_grad()
        loss = (1 - p[0]) ** 2 + 100 * (p[1] - p[0] ** 2) ** 2
        loss.backward(); opt.step(); out.append(p.detach().numpy().copy())
    return np.array(out)

19.10 AdamW: weight decay done properly

Weight decay shrinks weights towards zero. For plain SGD it equals adding \(\lambda\theta\) to the gradient (the L2 penalty of Chapter 28). With Adam it does not: the added gradient passes through the adaptive scaling, which changes its effect.

AdamW applies the decay directly to the weights:

\[ \theta_t=\theta_{t-1}-\eta\left(\frac{\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t}{\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t}+\epsilon}+\lambda\,\theta_{t-1}\right). \tag{19.6}\]

We see the difference with a parameter that has no loss gradient at all, only decay.

def decay_run(coupled, steps=100, lr=0.01, lam=0.5):
    out = {}
    for start in [1.0, 5.0]:
        p, m, v = start, 0.0, 0.0
        for t in range(1, steps + 1):
            g = lam * p if coupled else 0.0               # L2 inside the gradient
            m = 0.9 * m + 0.1 * g; v = 0.999 * v + 0.001 * g * g
            step = (m / (1 - 0.9 ** t)) / (np.sqrt(v / (1 - 0.999 ** t)) + 1e-8)
            p = p - lr * step - (0 if coupled else lr * lam * p)
        out[start] = p
    return out

print("start values 1.0 and 5.0, 100 steps, no loss gradient")
print("  Adam + L2 (coupled)  :", {k: round(float(v), 3) for k, v in decay_run(True).items()})
print("  AdamW    (decoupled) :", {k: round(float(v), 3) for k, v in decay_run(False).items()})
start values 1.0 and 5.0, 100 steps, no loss gradient
  Adam + L2 (coupled)  : {1.0: 0.224, 5.0: 4.036}
  AdamW    (decoupled) : {1.0: 0.606, 5.0: 3.029}

With coupled decay, both weights fall by almost the same absolute amount (about 0.8 and 1.0 here, roughly \(\eta\) per step), whatever their size, because Adam normalises the tiny gradient to a unit step. AdamW shrinks each weight by the same fraction (both end at 61% of their start). This is the behaviour that “weight decay” means, which is why AdamW is the standard for Transformers.

19.11 Failure modes

Learning rate too large. The loss grows or becomes nan. Reduce \(\eta\) or add warmup and gradient clipping (Chapter 20).

Learning rate too small. Training is slow and may look converged. A learning-rate sweep on a log scale is the first diagnostic.

Epsilon. In Adam, \(\epsilon\) prevents division by zero. For very small gradients, a large \(\epsilon\) makes the optimiser behave like SGD. In 16-bit arithmetic, \(10^{-8}\) underflows to zero.

Weight decay on the wrong parameters. Biases and normalisation scales are normally excluded from decay.

No warmup. Large models with Adam can diverge in the first steps, before the second-moment estimates are reliable.

Batch size and learning rate. Larger batches give less noise. They usually allow, and need, a larger learning rate. The rule of thumb is to scale \(\eta\) linearly with \(B\) up to a limit.

Generalisation. An optimiser that fits the training set faster does not always generalise better. Some settings favour SGD with momentum for vision models.

19.13 System connection

NoteWhat the system gained in this chapter

The system can now move. It has update rules that use momentum, adapt each parameter’s step, and follow a schedule. Together with the gradient (Chapter 18), the learning loop of Chapter 1 is complete and practical:

\[ x\to f_\theta(x)\to L\to\nabla_\theta L\to\theta'. \]

What remains is understanding when this loop works, and why it sometimes does not. Chapter 20 studies stability, conditioning, initialisation, and normalisation.

Chapter summary

  • GD: \(\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta g\). On a quadratic, it is stable only for \(\eta<2/\lambda_{\max}\).
  • SGD uses a minibatch gradient: unbiased, with variance \(\propto1/B\).
  • Momentum: \(v\leftarrow\beta v+g\). It accelerates consistent directions and damps oscillation.
  • RMSProp divides by the running RMS of the gradient. Adam adds momentum and bias correction.
  • The bias correction \(1/(1-\beta^t)\) compensates for the zero start.
  • AdamW decouples weight decay from the adaptive step.
  • Warmup and cosine decay are standard schedules.
