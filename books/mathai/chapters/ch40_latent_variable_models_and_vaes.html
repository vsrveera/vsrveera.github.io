40  Latent-Variable Models and VAEs

NoteKey ideas
  • A latent-variable model explains data \(\mathbf{x}\) through hidden variables \(\mathbf{z}\): sample \(\mathbf{z}\sim p(\mathbf{z})\), then \(\mathbf{x}\sim p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})\).
  • The likelihood \(p(\mathbf{x})=\int p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})p(\mathbf{z})\,d\mathbf{z}\) is intractable. The ELBO is a tractable lower bound: reconstruction minus a KL term.
  • A VAE uses an encoder \(q_\phi(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})\) (Gaussian), a decoder, and the reparameterisation trick to train both by gradient descent.
  • The KL term has a closed form for Gaussians and keeps the latent space organised, so that samples from the prior decode to plausible data.
  • Too strong a KL weight gives posterior collapse. Too weak a weight gives an autoencoder with a latent space full of holes.

40.1 The system problem

Everything so far understands data. A generative model must also produce it: sample new digits, images, or sentences. The plan of this chapter: assume that each data point is produced from a small number of hidden causes.

How do we define and train a model that generates data from hidden variables?

40.2 Latent-variable models

Suppose a handwritten digit is determined by a few hidden factors: which digit, slant, thickness. We never observe them. A latent-variable model says:

\[ \mathbf{z}\sim p(\mathbf{z}),\qquad \mathbf{x}\sim p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z}). \tag{40.1}\]

The prior \(p(\mathbf{z})\) is simple (a standard Gaussian). The decoder \(p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})\) is a neural network that turns a latent code into a distribution over data. Generating is easy: sample \(\mathbf{z}\), decode.

Training by maximum likelihood (Chapter 25) needs the marginal likelihood

\[ p_\theta(\mathbf{x})=\int p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})\,p(\mathbf{z})\,d\mathbf{z}. \tag{40.2}\]

The integral runs over all latent codes and has no closed form for a neural-network decoder. We cannot compute \(\ln p_\theta(\mathbf{x})\), let alone its gradient.

40.3 The evidence lower bound

We introduce an approximate posterior \(q_\phi(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})\), the encoder: a distribution over codes that plausibly produced \(\mathbf{x}\).

TipDerivation: the ELBO

Multiply and divide inside the integral by \(q_\phi\), and use Jensen’s inequality for the concave \(\ln\) (Chapter 15): \[ \ln p_\theta(\mathbf{x})=\ln\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})}\!\Bigl[\frac{p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})\,p(\mathbf{z})}{q_\phi(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})}\Bigr] \;\ge\;\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}\!\bigl[\ln p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})\bigr]-D_{\text{KL}}\bigl(q_\phi(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})\,\Vert\,p(\mathbf{z})\bigr). \] The right side is the ELBO. The gap is exact: expanding with Bayes’ rule, \[ \ln p_\theta(\mathbf{x})=\text{ELBO}+D_{\text{KL}}\bigl(q_\phi(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})\,\Vert\,p_\theta(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})\bigr). \] The KL divergence is non-negative (Gibbs, Chapter 15), so the ELBO is a lower bound, and it is tight when \(q_\phi\) equals the true posterior. \(\blacksquare\)

The ELBO has two terms with clear meanings:

Term Meaning Pulls towards
\(\mathbb{E}_q[\ln p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})]\) reconstruction: can the decoder rebuild \(\mathbf{x}\) from the code? informative codes
\(-D_{\text{KL}}(q_\phi\Vert p)\) regularisation: stay close to the prior an organised, samplable space

Maximising the ELBO over \(\theta\) and \(\phi\) trains the model.

40.4 Making it trainable

Encoder. A neural network outputs the mean \(\boldsymbol{\mu}(\mathbf{x})\) and log-variance \(\ln\boldsymbol{\sigma}^2(\mathbf{x})\) of a diagonal Gaussian: \(q_\phi(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\text{diag}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}^2)\).

KL term, in closed form. For this Gaussian and the prior \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})\) (Chapter 23):

\[ D_{\text{KL}}=\frac12\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bigl(\mu_k^2+\sigma_k^2-1-\ln\sigma_k^{2}\bigr). \tag{40.3}\]

Reconstruction term, by sampling. The expectation over \(q_\phi\) is estimated with one sample. To let gradients reach the encoder, the sample is reparameterised (Chapter 23):

\[ \mathbf{z}=\boldsymbol{\mu}+\boldsymbol{\sigma}\odot\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\qquad\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}). \tag{40.4}\]

Decoder likelihood. With a Gaussian \(p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})=\mathcal{N}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{z}),s^{2}\mathbf{I})\), the log-likelihood is a squared error (Chapter 25): \(-\frac{\lVert\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}\rVert^2}{2s^2}-\frac D2\ln(2\pi s^2)\).

The loss to minimise is then (a negative ELBO, up to a constant):

\[ \mathcal{L}=\frac{\lVert\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}\rVert^2}{2s^2}+\beta\,D_{\text{KL}},\qquad\beta=1\text{ for the true ELBO}. \tag{40.5}\]

import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
D = load_digits(); X, y = D.data / 16.0, D.target
Xtr, Xte, ytr, yte = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25, random_state=0, stratify=y)
Xt, Xe = torch.tensor(Xtr, dtype=torch.float32), torch.tensor(Xte, dtype=torch.float32)
S_DEC = 0.25                                                   # decoder standard deviation s

class VAE(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, k=2, h=128):
        super().__init__()
        self.enc = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(64, h), nn.ReLU())
        self.mu, self.logvar = nn.Linear(h, k), nn.Linear(h, k)
        self.dec = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(k, h), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(h, 64), nn.Sigmoid())
    def encode(self, x):
        h = self.enc(x); return self.mu(h), self.logvar(h)
    def forward(self, x):
        mu, logvar = self.encode(x)
        z = mu + torch.exp(0.5 * logvar) * torch.randn_like(mu)        # reparameterisation
        return self.dec(z), mu, logvar

def parts(model, x):
    xh, mu, logvar = model(x)
    rec = ((x - xh) ** 2).sum(1) / (2 * S_DEC ** 2)                    # -ln p(x|z) up to a constant
    kl = 0.5 * (mu ** 2 + logvar.exp() - 1 - logvar).sum(1)
    return rec.mean(), kl.mean()

def train_vae(beta=1.0, k=2, steps=1500, seed=0):
    torch.manual_seed(seed)
    m = VAE(k); opt = torch.optim.Adam(m.parameters(), lr=2e-3)
    for _ in range(steps):
        i = torch.randint(0, len(Xt), (128,))
        rec, kl = parts(m, Xt[i]); loss = rec + beta * kl
        opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    return m

vae = train_vae(beta=1.0)
with torch.no_grad():
    rec, kl = parts(vae, Xe)
print(f"test set: reconstruction term {rec:.1f}, KL {kl:.2f} nats")
test set: reconstruction term 19.8, KL 3.92 nats

40.5.1 Sampling

To generate, sample \(\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})\) and decode. The KL term is what makes this work: it keeps the aggregate of the encoder’s outputs close to the prior, so that prior samples land where the decoder has been trained.

40.6 Checking the bound

The ELBO is a lower bound on \(\ln p_\theta(\mathbf{x})\). We can estimate the true log-likelihood of one image by importance sampling: draw many codes from \(q_\phi\) and average the ratios \(p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})p(\mathbf{z})/q(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})\) (Chapter 26). If our derivation is right, the estimate must lie above the ELBO.

def log_normal(x, mu, var):                                    # sum over the last dimension
    return (-0.5 * (math.log(2 * math.pi) + var.log() + (x - mu) ** 2 / var)).sum(-1)

@torch.no_grad()
def elbo_and_logp(model, x, n_samples=5000):
    mu, logvar = model.encode(x[None]); std = torch.exp(0.5 * logvar)
    z = mu + std * torch.randn(n_samples, mu.shape[1])
    xh = model.dec(z)
    log_px_z = log_normal(x[None], xh, torch.full_like(xh, S_DEC ** 2))     # normalised Gaussian
    log_pz = log_normal(z, torch.zeros_like(z), torch.ones_like(z))
    log_qz = log_normal(z, mu, std ** 2)
    w = log_px_z + log_pz - log_qz                              # log importance weights
    logp = torch.logsumexp(w, 0) - math.log(n_samples)
    elbo = w.mean()                                             # E_q[log p(x|z) + log p(z) − log q(z|x)]
    return elbo.item(), logp.item()

print(f"{'image':>6s} {'ELBO':>10s} {'log p(x) (IS)':>14s} {'gap':>8s}")
for i in range(5):
    e, lp = elbo_and_logp(vae, Xe[i])
    print(f"{i:6d} {e:10.2f} {lp:14.2f} {lp - e:8.2f}")

The importance-sampled \(\ln p(\mathbf{x})\) is above the ELBO in every row. The gap is \(D_{\text{KL}}(q\Vert p(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}))\): how far the encoder is from the true posterior.

40.7 The role of the KL weight

We train the same model with different weights \(\beta\) on the KL term (\(\beta=1\) is the true ELBO). We record the test reconstruction error and the KL, and the active dimensions: latent coordinates whose encoder mean varies across inputs.

betas = [0.0, 0.1, 1.0, 5.0, 20.0, 100.0]
rows = []
for b in betas:
    m = train_vae(beta=b, k=8, steps=1500)
    with torch.no_grad():
        rec_, kl_ = parts(m, Xe)
        mu_, _ = m.encode(Xe)
        active = int((mu_.var(0) > 0.05).sum())
    rows.append((b, rec_.item(), kl_.item(), active))
print(f"{'beta':>6s} {'recon error':>12s} {'KL':>8s} {'active dims (of 8)':>19s}")
for b, r_, k_, a_ in rows:
    print(f"{b:6.1f} {r_:12.1f} {k_:8.2f} {a_:19d}")
fig, ax1 = plt.subplots(figsize=(6, 3.5))
bs = [max(b, 0.02) for b, *_ in rows]
ax1.semilogx(bs, [r[1] for r in rows], "o-", color=PALETTE["loss"], label="reconstruction error")

The trade-off is the rate–distortion trade-off of information theory (Chapter 15): the KL is the rate (information the code carries about the input) and the reconstruction error is the distortion.

40.8 Production

torch.distributions provides the Gaussian, its rsample, and the KL. We check that our closed form and our sampling agree.

from torch.distributions import Normal, kl_divergence
torch.manual_seed(0)
mu_t, logvar_t = torch.randn(5, 4), torch.randn(5, 4)
q = Normal(mu_t, torch.exp(0.5 * logvar_t)); p = Normal(torch.zeros(5, 4), torch.ones(5, 4))
kl_lib = kl_divergence(q, p).sum(1)
kl_mine = 0.5 * (mu_t ** 2 + logvar_t.exp() - 1 - logvar_t).sum(1)
print("closed-form KL equals torch.distributions:", torch.allclose(kl_lib, kl_mine, atol=1e-5))
z = q.rsample()
print("rsample keeps the graph (gradients can flow):", z.requires_grad or mu_t.requires_grad is False)

40.10 Failure modes

Posterior collapse. The encoder outputs the prior for every input. The KL is near zero and the decoder ignores \(\mathbf{z}\). It occurs with large \(\beta\) or a very powerful decoder (an autoregressive decoder can model the data without the code). Remedies: KL annealing (increase \(\beta\) gradually), free bits, weaker decoders.

Holes. With \(\beta\to0\), the model is an autoencoder. Codes of different inputs sit far apart and the space between them decodes to nonsense. Samples from the prior fall in the holes.

Blurry samples. A Gaussian decoder with fixed variance reconstructs the mean of all images that fit a code. Fine detail is averaged away. Better likelihoods or adversarial and diffusion decoders (Chapters 41, 42) address it.

Wrong decoder variance. The scale \(s\) sets the balance between reconstruction and KL: it plays the role of \(\beta\). A poor choice looks like a poor \(\beta\).

Looseness of the bound. The ELBO may be far below \(\ln p(\mathbf{x})\) if the encoder family is too simple (diagonal Gaussian).

Latent dimension. Too few dimensions limit capacity. Too many go unused (inactive dimensions).

40.12 System connection

NoteWhat the system gained in this chapter

The system can now generate: it has a probabilistic model with hidden causes, a trainable bound on the likelihood (the ELBO), an encoder that infers causes, and a sampler (prior, then decoder). It also has a language for the trade-off between how much a code says and how well it reconstructs.

One latent step generates blurry samples. Chapter 41 chains many small latent steps, each denoising, and obtains sharp ones.

Chapter summary

  • A latent-variable model: \(\mathbf{z}\sim p(\mathbf{z})\), \(\mathbf{x}\sim p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})\). The marginal is intractable.
  • \(\text{ELBO}=\mathbb{E}_q[\ln p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})]-D_{\text{KL}}(q\Vert p)\le\ln p(\mathbf{x})\), with gap \(D_{\text{KL}}(q\Vert p(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}))\).
  • VAE: Gaussian encoder, reparameterisation \(\mathbf{z}=\boldsymbol{\mu}+\boldsymbol{\sigma}\odot\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\), closed-form KL.
  • Sampling: \(\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})\), decode.
  • \(\beta\) trades rate for distortion. Large \(\beta\) collapses the posterior. \(\beta=0\) gives holes.

  7. Code. Interpolate between the codes of two test digits and decode eight points. Plot them.
