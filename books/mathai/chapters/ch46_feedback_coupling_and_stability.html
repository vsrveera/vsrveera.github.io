46  Feedback, Coupling and Stability

NoteKey ideas
  • A feedback loop returns part of the output to the input. It can correct errors (negative feedback) or amplify them (positive feedback).
  • In a chain, a small input error can grow or shrink at each stage. The product of stage gains bounds the growth.
  • Systems that train on their own outputs can lose diversity. The effect is quiet and the loss is measured only against the original data.
  • A loop with delay and too much gain becomes unstable, even if each part is simple and linear.
  • Stability is a property of the loop, not of one component.

46.1 The system problem

Chapter 45 treated the system as a chain: input in, answer out. Real AI systems have loops. A recommender shows items, users click, the clicks become training data. A language model writes text, the text goes to the web, the web becomes training data. A controller measures a state, acts, and measures again.

When the output of a system affects its next input, what stays stable and what does not?

This chapter has three parts: how errors move along a chain, how they move around a loop, and how a loop with delay can oscillate.

46.2 Errors along a chain: gain

Take a chain \(y=C_m\circ\cdots\circ C_1(x)\) and add a small error \(\boldsymbol\epsilon\) to \(x\). To first order, the output error is the Jacobian of the chain times \(\boldsymbol\epsilon\) (Chapter 17). The chain rule (Chapter 18) gives

\[ \Delta\mathbf y\approx\mathbf J_m\mathbf J_{m-1}\cdots\mathbf J_1\,\boldsymbol\epsilon . \tag{46.1}\]

For the norm, the submultiplicative rule gives a bound:

\[ \|\Delta\mathbf y\|\le\Bigl(\prod_{k=1}^{m}\|\mathbf J_k\|_2\Bigr)\|\boldsymbol\epsilon\|. \tag{46.2}\]

Each factor \(\|\mathbf J_k\|_2\) is the gain of one stage: the largest stretch it applies to any error. If every gain is below 1, errors shrink. If one gain is above 1, a bad input can grow. The bound is loose: a random error does not usually point along the worst direction.

rng = np.random.default_rng(46)
n = 20
def stage(scale):
    """A random linear stage whose largest singular value is `scale`."""
    A = rng.normal(size=(n, n))
    return A * (scale / np.linalg.norm(A, 2))

for scale in (0.9, 1.0, 1.2):
    ratios, bounds = [], []
    for trial in range(200):
        Ms = [stage(scale) for _ in range(8)]
        eps = rng.normal(size=n)
        e = eps.copy()
        for M in Ms:
            e = M @ e
        ratios.append(np.linalg.norm(e) / np.linalg.norm(eps))
        bounds.append(np.prod([np.linalg.norm(M, 2) for M in Ms]))
    print(f"stage gain {scale}: bound {np.mean(bounds):8.3f}   actual mean {np.mean(ratios):8.3f}   "
          f"actual / bound {np.mean(ratios) / np.mean(bounds):.3f}")
stage gain 0.9: bound    0.430   actual mean    0.003   actual / bound 0.006
stage gain 1.0: bound    1.000   actual mean    0.006   actual / bound 0.006
stage gain 1.2: bound    4.300   actual mean    0.026   actual / bound 0.006

Eight stages with gain \(1.2\) have a bound of \(1.2^8\approx4.3\). The actual mean growth is \(0.026\), about \(0.6\%\) of the bound. A random matrix stretches only a few directions by its largest gain, and a random error is not aligned with them. Even with gain \(1.2\) the typical error shrinks. The bound is still the right warning: it says what can happen if the error is aligned. Adversarial inputs (Chapter 20) are the aligned case.

NoteGain and conditioning

The ratio of the largest to the smallest stretch of a matrix is its condition number (Chapter 5). A stage with a large condition number loses information in the squeezed directions and amplifies noise when the stage is inverted.

46.3 Errors around a loop: feedback

In a loop, the output of generation \(g\) is the input of generation \(g+1\). Take the simplest case. A model is a Gaussian \(\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)\). At each generation we draw \(n\) samples from the current model and refit the mean and standard deviation to them. The original data are never seen again.

The maximum-likelihood estimate of the variance is biased low by the factor \((n-1)/n\) (Chapter 25). The sample mean also moves by chance. Each generation adds an estimation error and no generation corrects it.

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
n_samples, n_gen, n_runs = 20, 100, 400
std = np.empty((n_runs, n_gen + 1)); std[:, 0] = 1.0
for r in range(n_runs):
    mu, sd = 0.0, 1.0
    for g in range(1, n_gen + 1):
        x = rng.normal(mu, sd, n_samples)
        mu, sd = x.mean(), x.std()          # maximum likelihood refit
        std[r, g] = sd
for g in (0, 10, 25, 50, 100):
    print(f"generation {g:3d}: mean std {std[:, g].mean():.3f}   median std {np.median(std[:, g]):.3f}")
generation   0: mean std 1.000   median std 1.000
generation  10: mean std 0.650   median std 0.568
generation  25: mean std 0.334   median std 0.252
generation  50: mean std 0.127   median std 0.068
generation 100: mean std 0.013   median std 0.004

The standard deviation drifts toward zero. The tails disappear first. Rare cases (large \(|x|\)) are the first to be lost, and the loss is not visible in the training loss of any single generation.

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.4, 3.4))
for r in range(25):
    ax.plot(std[r], color=PALETTE["data"], alpha=0.25, lw=0.8)
ax.plot(np.median(std, axis=0), color=PALETTE["loss"], lw=2.4, label="median of 400 runs")
ax.set_xlabel("generation"); ax.set_ylabel("fitted standard deviation"); ax.set_yscale("log")
ax.legend()
plt.show()

A remedy is to keep contact with the world: mix fresh real data into every generation. The next code cell mixes in a fraction \(\alpha\) of the original distribution.

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
def final_std(alpha, n_gen=100, n_runs=300):
    out = []
    for _ in range(n_runs):
        mu, sd = 0.0, 1.0
        for _ in range(n_gen):
            k = int(round(alpha * n_samples))
            x = np.concatenate([rng.normal(mu, sd, n_samples - k), rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, k)])
            mu, sd = x.mean(), x.std()
        out.append(sd)
    return np.mean(out)
for a in (0.0, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5):
    print(f"fresh real data fraction {a:4.2f}: mean std after 100 generations {final_std(a):.3f}")
fresh real data fraction 0.00: mean std after 100 generations 0.018
fresh real data fraction 0.10: mean std after 100 generations 0.841
fresh real data fraction 0.25: mean std after 100 generations 0.913
fresh real data fraction 0.50: mean std after 100 generations 0.962

Without fresh data the mean spread after 100 generations is \(0.018\). With 10% fresh data it is \(0.84\), and with 50% it is \(0.96\). The true value is \(1\). A small fraction of real data is enough to stop most of the loss, but it does not remove the bias completely. The principle applies to any loop: an anchor to ground truth keeps the loop from drifting. In production, the anchor is a flow of fresh, independently labelled examples.

46.4 Delay and gain: oscillation

Feedback control corrects an error in proportion to its size. Let \(x_t\) be the deviation from a target. We apply a correction of strength \(k\), but the measurement is late by one step:

\[ x_{t+1}=x_t-k\,x_{t-1}. \tag{46.3}\]

The solutions of Equation 46.3 grow like \(z^t\), where \(z\) solves \(z^2-z+k=0\). For \(k>1/4\) the roots are complex with \(|z|^2=k\). So the deviation decays if \(k<1\) and grows if \(k>1\). A correction that is too strong, applied with a delay, overshoots and makes the error larger.

def run(k, T=40):
    x = np.zeros(T); x[0] = x[1] = 1.0
    for t in range(1, T - 1):
        x[t + 1] = x[t] - k * x[t - 1]
    return x
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.6, 3.4))
for k, c in [(0.5, PALETTE["grad"]), (1.0, PALETTE["model"]), (1.2, PALETTE["loss"])]:
    ax.plot(run(k), color=c, lw=2, label=f"gain k = {k}")
ax.set_xlabel("time step t"); ax.set_ylabel("deviation $x_t$"); ax.set_ylim(-4, 4)
ax.legend(loc="upper left")
plt.show()
for k in (0.5, 1.0, 1.2):
    r = np.roots([1, -1, k])
    print(f"k={k}: largest root magnitude {np.abs(r).max():.3f}   |x_40| = {abs(run(k, 41)[-1]):.3f}")
k=0.5: largest root magnitude 0.707   |x_40| = 0.000
k=1.0: largest root magnitude 1.000   |x_40| = 1.000
k=1.2: largest root magnitude 1.095   |x_40| = 35.951

The numbers agree with the analysis: the largest root has magnitude \(\sqrt k\).

The same pattern appears far from control engineering. A learning rate that is too high (Chapter 19) is a gain above the stability limit. An autoscaler that reacts to a slow metric oscillates. A pricing model that reacts to its own effect on demand overshoots.

46.5 Coupling

Two components are coupled when a change in one changes the correct setting of the other. Chapter 45 named one form: an upstream threshold tuned for recall changes the downstream workload. Coupling makes local tuning unsafe. Each team improves its own score and the whole gets worse.

We handle coupling in three ways.

  1. Decouple with an interface that hides the change (a stable contract, Chapter 45).
  2. Tune jointly against the system measure (Chapter 50).
  3. Monitor the interface distribution, so that a drift is seen when it starts (Chapter 27).

46.6 Failure modes

Self-training drift. A system trains on its own labels or outputs. Diversity and tails vanish (Figure 46.2).

Popularity loops. A recommender shows what is clicked. What is shown is clicked more. Items never shown cannot be measured.

Over-correction. A controller or a tuner reacts to noise as if it were signal. Gain with delay gives oscillation (Figure 46.3).

Hidden coupling. One team changes a pre-processing step. A downstream model silently loses accuracy.

Aligned perturbation. A small error aligned with a high-gain direction is amplified (Equation 46.2). Natural noise rarely does this. An adversary does.

46.8 System connection

NoteWhat the system gained in this chapter

The system can now be analysed as a dynamic object. We have a bound on how a chain amplifies a small error, a worked example of a loop that forgets its tails, a rule for when a delayed correction turns into oscillation, and a name for coupling.

All of this reasoning used qualitative tools and small simulations. We still lack a standard procedure to say, for a given component, how its output error depends on its input noise and its parameters. That is performance characterisation. Chapter 47 gives its paradigm.

Chapter summary

  • The output error of a chain is the product of Jacobians applied to the input error. The product of stage gains bounds it.
  • A loop that refits on its own outputs loses variance and tails. Fresh real data hold it.
  • Delay plus too much gain gives oscillation or growth. The boundary can be computed.
  • Coupling means local tuning can hurt the whole.
  • Stability belongs to the loop.
