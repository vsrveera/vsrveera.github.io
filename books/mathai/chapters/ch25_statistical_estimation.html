25  Statistical Estimation

NoteKey ideas
  • The likelihood is the probability of the observed data as a function of the parameters. Maximum likelihood picks the parameters that make the data most probable.
  • Assuming Gaussian noise, maximum likelihood is least squares. Assuming categorical outputs, it is cross-entropy.
  • MAP estimation adds a prior. A Gaussian prior gives L2 regularisation (weight decay). A Laplace prior gives L1.
  • A full Bayesian treatment keeps the whole posterior, and gives uncertainty bands.
  • The chain probability model → likelihood → loss → optimisation is how every loss in this book is derived.

25.1 The system problem

We used squared error, cross-entropy, and weight decay without saying where they come from. They looked like design choices. They are not arbitrary.

Why are cross-entropy and mean squared error the right losses, and why does weight decay appear?

The answer is a single principle: choose the parameters under which the observed data is most probable.

25.2 Likelihood and maximum likelihood

A probabilistic model gives the probability (or density) of data \(\mathcal{D}\) for each setting of the parameters \(\theta\): \(p(\mathcal{D}\mid\theta)\). Seen as a function of \(\theta\), with the data fixed, it is the likelihood \(\mathcal{L}(\theta)\).

For independent examples, it is a product, so we work with its logarithm:

\[ \ell(\theta)=\ln\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ln p(y_{i}\mid x_i;\theta). \tag{25.1}\]

The maximum-likelihood estimate (MLE) is

\[ \hat\theta_{\text{MLE}}=\arg\max_\theta\ \ell(\theta)=\arg\min_\theta\ \underbrace{-\frac1N\sum_{i}\ln p(y_{i}\mid x_i;\theta)}_{\text{negative log-likelihood (NLL)}}. \tag{25.2}\]

Maximising the likelihood is the same as minimising the negative log-likelihood, which is a loss function.

25.2.1 Example: a coin

We flip a coin \(n=10\) times and see \(k=7\) heads. For heads probability \(\theta\), \(\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\theta^k(1-\theta)^{n-k}\) and \(\ell(\theta)=k\ln\theta+(n-k)\ln(1-\theta)\). Setting the derivative to zero:

\[ \frac{k}{\theta}-\frac{n-k}{1-\theta}=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\hat\theta=\frac{k}{n}. \]

The MLE is the observed frequency.

k, n = 7, 10
th = np.linspace(0.01, 0.99, 400)
L = th ** k * (1 - th) ** (n - k)
fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9, 3.2))
ax1.plot(th, L, color=PALETTE["data"], lw=2); ax1.axvline(k / n, ls="--", color=PALETTE["loss"])
ax1.set_xlabel("θ"); ax1.set_ylabel("likelihood")
ax2.plot(th, np.log(L), color=PALETTE["model"], lw=2); ax2.axvline(k / n, ls="--", color=PALETTE["loss"])
ax2.set_xlabel("θ"); ax2.set_ylabel("log-likelihood")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
print(f"numerical argmax: {th[np.argmax(L)]:.3f}   closed form k/n = {k / n}")
numerical argmax: 0.700   closed form k/n = 0.7

25.2.2 Example: a Gaussian

For \(y_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)\), the log-likelihood is \(-\frac N2\ln(2\pi\sigma^2)-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i(y_i-\mu)^2\). The derivative in \(\mu\) gives \(\hat\mu=\frac1N\sum_iy_i\), the sample mean. The derivative in \(\sigma^2\) gives \(\hat\sigma^2=\frac1N\sum_i(y_i-\hat\mu)^2\). (This variance estimate is slightly biased low, as Chapter 26 shows.)

25.3 From likelihood to loss

The likelihood of a model that predicts \(y\) from \(x\) depends on what we assume about the randomness in \(y\).

25.3.1 Gaussian noise gives squared error

Suppose \(y=f_\theta(x)+\varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)\). Then \(p(y\mid x;\theta)=\mathcal{N}(y;f_\theta(x),\sigma^2)\) and

\[ -\ln p(y_{i}\mid x_i;\theta)=\frac{(y_i-f_\theta(x_i))^2}{2\sigma^2}+\frac12\ln(2\pi\sigma^2). \tag{25.3}\]

The second term does not depend on \(\theta\). Summing over examples, the NLL is the mean squared error scaled by \(1/2\sigma^2\), plus a constant. Minimising MSE is maximum likelihood under Gaussian noise.

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
N = 50
x = rng.uniform(-2, 2, N); y = 2 * x + 1 + rng.normal(0, 0.5, N)
sigma = 0.5

def nll(a, b):                                    # sum over examples of -ln N(y; a x + b, sigma²)
    r = y - (a * x + b)
    return np.sum(0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi * sigma ** 2) + r ** 2 / (2 * sigma ** 2))
mse = lambda a, b: np.mean((y - (a * x + b)) ** 2)

print(f"{'(a, b)':>12s} {'NLL':>9s} {'MSE':>8s} {'NLL − N/(2σ²)·MSE':>20s}")
for a, b in [(0.0, 0.0), (1.0, 0.5), (2.0, 1.0), (3.0, -1.0)]:
    print(f"{str((a, b)):>12s} {nll(a, b):9.2f} {mse(a, b):8.3f} "
          f"{nll(a, b) - N / (2 * sigma ** 2) * mse(a, b):20.3f}")
print(f"constant N/2·ln(2πσ²) = {N / 2 * np.log(2 * np.pi * sigma ** 2):.3f}")
      (a, b)       NLL      MSE    NLL − N/(2σ²)·MSE
  (0.0, 0.0)    767.53    7.562               11.290
  (1.0, 0.5)    229.33    2.180               11.290
  (2.0, 1.0)     36.73    0.254               11.290
 (3.0, -1.0)    519.36    5.081               11.290
constant N/2·ln(2πσ²) = 11.290

The last column is the same for every \((a,b)\). The NLL and the MSE differ only by a scale and a constant, so they have the same minimiser.

25.3.2 Categorical outputs give cross-entropy

For classification, the model outputs a probability vector \(\mathbf{q}_\theta(x)\) over \(K\) classes. The likelihood of the label \(y_i\) is \(q_{\theta,y_i}(x_i)\), so

\[ \text{NLL}=-\frac1N\sum_{i}\ln q_{\theta,y_i}(x_i). \tag{25.4}\]

This is the cross-entropy loss of Chapters 14 and 24. The cross-entropy of Chapter 15 makes the link exact. Let \(\hat{p}\) be the empirical distribution of the data. Then

\[ \text{NLL}=H(\hat{p},q_\theta)=H(\hat{p})+D_{\text{KL}}(\hat{p}\Vert q_\theta). \]

Since \(H(\hat{p})\) does not depend on \(\theta\), maximum likelihood is the same as minimising the KL divergence from the data distribution to the model. We verify the identity for a discrete example.

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
labels = rng.choice(3, size=1000, p=[0.6, 0.3, 0.1])
p_hat = np.bincount(labels, minlength=3) / len(labels)          # empirical distribution
q = np.array([0.5, 0.35, 0.15])                                  # some model
nll_cat = -np.mean(np.log(q[labels]))                            # mean NLL
H = -np.sum(p_hat * np.log(p_hat)); KL = np.sum(p_hat * np.log(p_hat / q))
print(f"NLL = {nll_cat:.5f};   H(p̂) + KL(p̂‖q) = {H + KL:.5f}")
print(f"the q that minimises NLL is q = p̂ = {np.round(p_hat, 3)};  its NLL equals H(p̂) = {H:.5f}")
NLL = 0.92875;   H(p̂) + KL(p̂‖q) = 0.92875
the q that minimises NLL is q = p̂ = [0.596 0.296 0.108];  its NLL equals H(p̂) = 0.90916

25.4 Priors: MAP estimation

MLE uses only the data. With few data points, it can be erratic. A prior \(p(\theta)\) encodes what we believe about the parameters before seeing data. By Bayes’ theorem (Chapter 22),

\[ p(\theta\mid\mathcal{D})\;\propto\;p(\mathcal{D}\mid\theta)\,p(\theta). \tag{25.5}\]

The maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate maximises the posterior:

\[ \hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}=\arg\min_\theta\Bigl[\underbrace{-\ln p(\mathcal{D}\mid\theta)}_{\text{data loss}}\;\underbrace{-\ln p(\theta)}_{\text{regulariser}}\Bigr]. \tag{25.6}\]

The prior becomes a penalty.

TipDerivation: a Gaussian prior is L2 regularisation

Take Gaussian noise \(\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)\) and a prior \(\mathbf{w}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\tau^{2}\mathbf{I})\). Then \[ -\ln p(\mathbf{w}\mid\mathcal{D})=\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i(y_i-\mathbf{w}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\phi}_i)^2+\frac{1}{2\tau^2}\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2+\text{const}. \] Multiplying by \(2\sigma^2\), the minimiser is that of \[ \sum_i(y_i-\mathbf{w}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\phi}_i)^2+\lambda\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2,\qquad\lambda=\frac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2}. \] This is ridge regression. The closed form is \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}=(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\Phi}+\lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\intercal}\mathbf{y}\). A small prior variance \(\tau^2\) (a strong belief that weights are small) means a large \(\lambda\). \(\blacksquare\)

A Laplace prior \(p(w)\propto e^{-|w|/b}\) gives a penalty \(\propto\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert_1\): L1 regularisation, which produces sparse solutions (Chapter 28).

25.4.1 Overfitting and its repair

We fit a degree-9 polynomial to 12 noisy points. The MLE (least squares) has enough freedom to pass through every point and swing wildly between them. The MAP estimate with a Gaussian prior is smoother.

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
f_true = lambda t: np.sin(2 * np.pi * t)
xt = np.sort(rng.uniform(0, 1, 12)); yt = f_true(xt) + rng.normal(0, 0.25, 12)
xg = np.linspace(0, 1, 300)
Phi = lambda t: np.vander(t, 10, increasing=True)             # degree 9
P, Pg = Phi(xt), Phi(xg)

def ridge(lam):
    return np.linalg.solve(P.T @ P + lam * np.eye(P.shape[1]), P.T @ yt)

w_mle = np.linalg.lstsq(P, yt, rcond=None)[0]
w_map = ridge(1e-3)
lams = np.logspace(-8, 1, 30)
test_err = [np.mean((Pg @ ridge(l) - f_true(xg)) ** 2) for l in lams]

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 3.6))
ax1.plot(xg, f_true(xg), "--", color="black", label="truth")
ax1.plot(xg, Pg @ w_mle, color=PALETTE["loss"], label="MLE (least squares)")
ax1.plot(xg, Pg @ w_map, color=PALETTE["grad"], lw=2, label="MAP (λ = 1e-3)")
ax1.scatter(xt, yt, color=PALETTE["data"], zorder=3); ax1.set_ylim(-2, 2); ax1.legend(fontsize=8)
ax2.loglog(lams, test_err, "o-", color=PALETTE["model"]); ax2.set_xlabel("λ = σ²/τ²")
ax2.set_ylabel("test mean squared error")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
print(f"‖w‖: MLE {np.linalg.norm(w_mle):.1f},  MAP {np.linalg.norm(w_map):.1f}")
print(f"test MSE: MLE {np.mean((Pg @ w_mle - f_true(xg)) ** 2):.3f}, MAP {np.mean((Pg @ w_map - f_true(xg)) ** 2):.3f}")
‖w‖: MLE 5109237.7,  MAP 9.8
test MSE: MLE 154233.495, MAP 0.071

We also check that ridge is the MAP estimate, by minimising the negative log-posterior numerically and by comparing with scikit-learn.

from scipy.optimize import minimize
from sklearn.linear_model import Ridge
lam = 1e-3
neg_log_post = lambda w: np.sum((yt - P @ w) ** 2) + lam * np.sum(w ** 2)
w_num = minimize(neg_log_post, np.zeros(10), method="BFGS", options={"gtol": 1e-10}).x
w_skl = Ridge(alpha=lam, fit_intercept=False).fit(P, yt).coef_
print("closed form vs numeric minimiser :", np.allclose(w_map, w_num, atol=1e-3))
print("closed form vs sklearn Ridge     :", np.allclose(w_map, w_skl, atol=1e-6))
closed form vs numeric minimiser : True
closed form vs sklearn Ridge     : True

25.4.2 L1: sparse solutions

With 20 features of which only 3 matter, the L1 penalty zeroes out most of the useless ones. The L2 penalty shrinks all of them but keeps them non-zero.

from sklearn.linear_model import Lasso, LinearRegression
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
Xs = rng.normal(size=(60, 20)); w_true = np.zeros(20); w_true[:3] = [3.0, -2.0, 1.5]
ys = Xs @ w_true + rng.normal(0, 1.0, 60)
ols = LinearRegression(fit_intercept=False).fit(Xs, ys).coef_
rdg = Ridge(alpha=30.0, fit_intercept=False).fit(Xs, ys).coef_
las = Lasso(alpha=0.25, fit_intercept=False).fit(Xs, ys).coef_
idx = np.arange(20)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(9, 3.4))
ax.bar(idx - 0.27, ols, 0.27, color=PALETTE["data"], label="least squares")
ax.bar(idx, rdg, 0.27, color=PALETTE["model"], label="ridge (L2)")
ax.bar(idx + 0.27, las, 0.27, color=PALETTE["grad"], label="lasso (L1)")
ax.plot(idx, w_true, "ko", ms=5, label="truth"); ax.legend(fontsize=8, ncol=4)
ax.set_xlabel("feature"); ax.set_ylabel("coefficient")
plt.show()
print("non-zero coefficients: least squares", int(np.sum(np.abs(ols) > 1e-8)),
      ", ridge", int(np.sum(np.abs(rdg) > 1e-8)), ", lasso", int(np.sum(np.abs(las) > 1e-8)))
non-zero coefficients: least squares 20 , ridge 20 , lasso 3

25.5 Keeping the whole posterior: Bayesian regression

MAP keeps only the peak of the posterior. For Bayesian linear regression (Gaussian noise and Gaussian prior), the posterior over \(\mathbf{w}\) is itself Gaussian, with

\[ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{post}}^{-1}=\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\Phi}+\frac{1}{\tau^2}\mathbf{I},\qquad \mathbf{m}_{\text{post}}=\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{post}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\intercal}\mathbf{y}. \tag{25.7}\]

The mean \(\mathbf{m}_{\text{post}}\) equals the ridge solution with \(\lambda=\sigma^2/\tau^2\). The covariance tells how uncertain each parameter is, and so how uncertain predictions are.

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
xb = rng.uniform(-2, 0, 6); yb = 1.5 * xb + 0.5 + rng.normal(0, 0.3, 6)
sig, tau = 0.3, 2.0
Phi_b = np.c_[xb, np.ones_like(xb)]                               # features: [x, 1]
S_inv = Phi_b.T @ Phi_b / sig ** 2 + np.eye(2) / tau ** 2
S_post = np.linalg.inv(S_inv)
m_post = S_post @ Phi_b.T @ yb / sig ** 2

xs = np.linspace(-3, 4, 200); Phi_s = np.c_[xs, np.ones_like(xs)]
mean = Phi_s @ m_post
std = np.sqrt(np.einsum("ij,jk,ik->i", Phi_s, S_post, Phi_s))
W = rng.multivariate_normal(m_post, S_post, size=30)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.6, 3.8))
ax.plot(xs, Phi_s @ W.T, color=PALETTE["data"], alpha=0.25, lw=1)
ax.fill_between(xs, mean - 2 * std, mean + 2 * std, color=PALETTE["model"], alpha=0.25)
ax.plot(xs, mean, color=PALETTE["model"], lw=2)
ax.scatter(xb, yb, color="black", zorder=3); ax.set_xlabel("x"); ax.set_ylabel("y")
plt.show()
print(f"predictive std at x = -1: {std[np.argmin(np.abs(xs + 1))]:.3f},  at x = 4: {std[-1]:.3f}")
ridge_w = np.linalg.solve(Phi_b.T @ Phi_b + (sig ** 2 / tau ** 2) * np.eye(2), Phi_b.T @ yb)
print("posterior mean equals ridge with λ = σ²/τ²:", np.allclose(m_post, ridge_w))
predictive std at x = -1: 0.123,  at x = 4: 1.129
posterior mean equals ridge with λ = σ²/τ²: True

25.6 Production

In deep learning, the losses above come straight from the framework. The prior shows up as weight decay in the optimiser (Chapter 19). We check the equivalence for a small model: adding \(\frac\lambda2\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2\) to the loss gives the same gradient as an SGD weight-decay term.

import torch
torch.manual_seed(0)
w = torch.randn(5, requires_grad=True); X = torch.randn(20, 5); t = torch.randn(20)
lam_wd = 0.1

loss = ((X @ w - t) ** 2).mean() + 0.5 * lam_wd * (w ** 2).sum()    # explicit L2 penalty
loss.backward(); g_penalty = w.grad.clone()

w.grad = None
((X @ w - t) ** 2).mean().backward()
g_decay = w.grad + lam_wd * w.detach()                              # what SGD weight_decay adds
print("penalty gradient == data gradient + λ w:", torch.allclose(g_penalty, g_decay))

nll_t = torch.nn.functional.gaussian_nll_loss(X @ w.detach(), t, torch.ones(20), full=True, reduction="sum")
by_hand = 0.5 * ((X @ w.detach() - t) ** 2).sum() + 20 * 0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi)
print("gaussian_nll_loss == ½Σr² + (N/2)ln 2π:", np.isclose(nll_t.item(), by_hand.item()))
penalty gradient == data gradient + λ w: True
gaussian_nll_loss == ½Σr² + (N/2)ln 2π: True

25.7 Failure modes

Overfitting. The MLE with a flexible model fits the noise (Figure 25.4). A prior, early stopping, or more data fixes it (Chapter 28).

Model misspecification. If the noise has heavy tails but we assume Gaussian, the MLE is pulled by outliers (Chapter 14). The “right” loss depends on the true noise.

A prior that is too strong. The posterior then ignores the data. State the prior and test how much conclusions depend on it.

A point estimate hides uncertainty. MLE and MAP give one value. They say nothing about how well the data determines it. With 6 points, the Bayesian band is wide; the point estimate looks as sure as with 6,000.

Biased variance. The MLE of the variance divides by \(N\). It underestimates the true variance (Chapter 26).

Likelihood is not a probability of the parameters. \(\mathcal{L}(\theta)\) does not integrate to 1 over \(\theta\). Only with a prior does the posterior become a distribution over \(\theta\).

Non-identifiable parameters. If different \(\theta\) give the same predictions (permuting hidden units), the MLE is not unique. This is typical of neural networks.

25.9 System connection

NoteWhat the system gained in this chapter

The system now has a principle for its losses. The loss is the negative log-likelihood of a probabilistic model. The regulariser is the negative log-prior. Cross-entropy, mean squared error, and weight decay are all consequences, not choices.

This closes Part VI. The system can represent, transform, measure, learn, and reason under uncertainty. One question is left: why should a model that does well on the data it has seen do well on data it has not? Part VII asks that, starting with what a finite sample can tell us.

Chapter summary

  • The likelihood is \(p(\mathcal{D}\mid\theta)\) as a function of \(\theta\). MLE minimises the NLL.
  • Gaussian noise gives squared error. Categorical outputs give cross-entropy. Laplace noise gives absolute error.
  • MLE equals minimising \(D_{\text{KL}}(\hat{p}\Vert q_\theta)\).
  • MAP adds \(-\ln p(\theta)\). Gaussian prior \(\to\) L2 (ridge, \(\lambda=\sigma^2/\tau^2\)). Laplace prior \(\to\) L1 (lasso).
  • Bayesian linear regression keeps the full posterior: mean equals ridge, covariance gives predictive uncertainty.
