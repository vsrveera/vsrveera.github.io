27  Generalization

NoteKey ideas
  • Empirical risk is the average loss on the training set. Expected risk is the average loss on the population. Learning aims at the second and can only measure the first.
  • For a model fixed in advance, the empirical risk is an unbiased estimate of the expected risk. For a model chosen because it scored well, it is biased low.
  • The generalisation gap grows with model flexibility and shrinks with data.
  • Validation and cross-validation estimate the expected risk honestly. The test set should be used once.
  • If the test distribution differs from the training distribution (shift), even a perfect training score can mislead.

27.1 The system problem

Training minimises the loss on the training set. We want a model that does well on new examples. These two are different quantities, and the first is only a stand-in for the second.

Training loss is low. Why should test loss be low too, and when is it not?

This chapter makes the question precise and shows both the good case and the failures.

27.2 Two risks

Let \(\ell(f(x),y)\) be the loss of predictor \(f\) on one example. The expected risk is its average over the population \(p(x,y)\):

\[ R(f)=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim p}\bigl[\ell(f(x),y)\bigr]. \tag{27.1}\]

We cannot compute it, because we do not have \(p\). We have a sample \(\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^N\) and compute the empirical risk:

\[ \hat R_N(f)=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell(f(x_i),y_i). \tag{27.2}\]

Training is empirical risk minimisation: \(\hat{f}=\arg\min_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\hat R_N(f)\). The generalisation gap is \(R(\hat f)-\hat R_N(\hat f)\).

27.2.1 A fixed predictor: no gap on average

If \(f\) was chosen before seeing the sample, then \(\hat R_N(f)\) is an average of \(N\) iid losses, and

\[ \mathbb{E}\bigl[\hat R_N(f)\bigr]=R(f). \tag{27.3}\]

The empirical risk is an unbiased estimate (Chapter 26). Its error is of order \(1/\sqrt N\).

27.2.2 A selected predictor: the optimiser’s curse

If \(\hat f\) was selected because it minimised \(\hat R_N\) over a class, the guarantee is lost. We select the one whose score was most lucky. Formally, for any \(f_{0}\in\mathcal{F}\),

\[ \mathbb{E}\bigl[\hat R_N(\hat f)\bigr]\le\mathbb{E}\bigl[\hat R_N(f_0)\bigr]=R(f_0). \tag{27.4}\]

The training risk of the chosen model is, on average, lower than the risk of any fixed member. The larger the class, the larger the effect. We see it with a class of pure guesses.

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n_sel, n_new = 100, 100_000
Ms = [1, 2, 5, 10, 30, 100, 300, 1000]
apparent, fresh = [], []
for M in Ms:
    a, f_ = [], []
    for _ in range(200):
        acc_sel = rng.binomial(n_sel, 0.5, size=M) / n_sel       # accuracy of each guesser
        best = acc_sel.argmax()
        a.append(acc_sel[best])
        f_.append(rng.binomial(n_new, 0.5) / n_new)               # chosen one, on fresh data
    apparent.append(np.mean(a)); fresh.append(np.mean(f_))
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6, 3.5))
ax.semilogx(Ms, apparent, "o-", color=PALETTE["loss"], label="apparent (selection sample)")
ax.semilogx(Ms, fresh, "o-", color=PALETTE["grad"], label="true (fresh data)")
ax.set_xlabel("number of candidate classifiers M"); ax.set_ylabel("accuracy"); ax.legend()
plt.show()
print("apparent accuracy of the best of M:", dict(zip(Ms, np.round(apparent, 3))))
apparent accuracy of the best of M: {1: np.float64(0.499), 2: np.float64(0.525), 5: np.float64(0.555), 10: np.float64(0.578), 30: np.float64(0.602), 100: np.float64(0.624), 300: np.float64(0.643), 1000: np.float64(0.661)}

The same effect arises when a researcher tries many hyperparameters, architectures, or random seeds and reports the best test score. The test set is then also a selection set.

27.3 Flexibility and the gap

A more flexible model can lower the training risk further. Past some point, it fits the noise. We see the classic picture with polynomial regression of growing degree. For each degree we average over many independent training sets.

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
f_true = lambda t: np.sin(2 * np.pi * t)
degrees = np.arange(0, 13)
n_train, sigma, reps = 20, 0.3, 200
xt_grid = np.linspace(0, 1, 400)
tr = np.zeros((reps, len(degrees))); te = np.zeros_like(tr)
for r in range(reps):
    x = rng.uniform(0, 1, n_train); y = f_true(x) + rng.normal(0, sigma, n_train)
    for j, d in enumerate(degrees):
        c = np.polynomial.polynomial.polyfit(2 * x - 1, y, d)
        tr[r, j] = np.mean((np.polynomial.polynomial.polyval(2 * x - 1, c) - y) ** 2)
        te[r, j] = np.mean((np.polynomial.polynomial.polyval(2 * xt_grid - 1, c) - f_true(xt_grid)) ** 2) + sigma ** 2
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.2, 3.6))
ax.semilogy(degrees, np.median(tr, axis=0), "o-", color=PALETTE["data"], label="training error")
ax.semilogy(degrees, np.median(te, axis=0), "o-", color=PALETTE["loss"], label="test error")
ax.axhline(sigma ** 2, ls="--", color=PALETTE["neutral"], label="noise floor σ²")
ax.set_xlabel("polynomial degree"); ax.set_ylabel("mean squared error (median)"); ax.legend()
plt.show()
print("best degree by test error:", int(degrees[np.argmin(np.median(te, axis=0))]))
print("best degree by training error:", int(degrees[np.argmin(np.median(tr, axis=0))]))
best degree by test error: 3
best degree by training error: 12

Selecting the degree by training error always chooses the largest model. We need an honest estimate.

27.4 Estimating the expected risk honestly

27.4.1 Hold-out validation

Split off a validation set the model never trains on. Its average loss is an unbiased estimate of \(R\) for the trained model (Equation 27.3 applies, since the model is fixed once trained). Use it to choose among models. Then evaluate the winner once on a separate test set.

27.4.2 Cross-validation

With little data, a single validation set is noisy and wasteful. In \(k\)-fold cross-validation we split the data into \(k\) parts, train on \(k-1\), validate on the remaining one, and rotate. The mean of the \(k\) scores uses all the data.

def kfold_indices(n, k, rng):
    idx = rng.permutation(n)
    return np.array_split(idx, k)

def cv_error(x, y, degree, k, rng):
    folds = kfold_indices(len(x), k, rng); errs = []
    for i in range(k):
        val = folds[i]; trn = np.concatenate([folds[j] for j in range(k) if j != i])
        c = np.polynomial.polynomial.polyfit(2 * x[trn] - 1, y[trn], degree)
        pred = np.polynomial.polynomial.polyval(2 * x[val] - 1, c)
        errs.append(np.mean((pred - y[val]) ** 2))
    return np.mean(errs)

rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
x = rng.uniform(0, 1, 30); y = f_true(x) + rng.normal(0, 0.3, 30)
cv = [cv_error(x, y, d, 5, rng) for d in range(0, 11)]
train_err = [np.mean((np.polynomial.polynomial.polyval(2 * x - 1,
              np.polynomial.polynomial.polyfit(2 * x - 1, y, d)) - y) ** 2) for d in range(0, 11)]
print(f"degree chosen by training error: {int(np.argmin(train_err))}")
print(f"degree chosen by 5-fold CV     : {int(np.argmin(cv))}")
degree chosen by training error: 10
degree chosen by 5-fold CV     : 3

27.4.3 The test set

After all choices are made, evaluate once on the test set. Each reuse turns the test set into a validation set and the estimate becomes optimistic (Figure 27.2).

27.5 Distribution shift

Everything above assumed that training and test examples come from the same distribution. In deployment this often fails. Three kinds of shift are standard:

Kind What changes Example
Covariate shift \(p(x)\) changes, \(p(y\mid x)\) stays a camera in a new lighting; a new user population
Label shift \(p(y)\) changes, \(p(x\mid y)\) stays disease prevalence differs between hospitals
Concept shift \(p(y\mid x)\) changes fraud patterns evolve; a word changes meaning

We illustrate covariate shift. A nonlinear truth is fitted by a straight line on data concentrated on the left. The line is acceptable there, because over a small range a curve looks straight. On the right, where no training data lay, it fails.

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
g = lambda t: np.sin(1.5 * t) + 0.3 * t
x_tr = rng.normal(0.0, 0.7, 200); y_tr = g(x_tr) + rng.normal(0, 0.1, 200)
x_te = rng.normal(2.5, 0.5, 200); y_te = g(x_te) + rng.normal(0, 0.1, 200)
c = np.polyfit(x_tr, y_tr, 1)
mse = lambda x, y: np.mean((np.polyval(c, x) - y) ** 2)
print(f"line fitted on training region: train MSE {mse(x_tr, y_tr):.3f}, shifted-test MSE {mse(x_te, y_te):.3f}")

xs = np.linspace(-2.5, 4, 300)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.4, 3.6))
ax.plot(xs, g(xs), color="black", label="truth")
ax.plot(xs, np.polyval(c, xs), color=PALETTE["loss"], lw=2, label="fitted line")
ax.scatter(x_tr, y_tr, s=6, color=PALETTE["data"], label="train inputs")
ax.scatter(x_te, y_te, s=6, color=PALETTE["model"], label="test inputs")
ax.legend(fontsize=8); ax.set_xlabel("x")
plt.show()
line fitted on training region: train MSE 0.055, shifted-test MSE 8.907

27.5.1 Correcting covariate shift by importance weighting

If we know both input densities, we can re-weight the training examples by \(w(x)=p_{\text{test}}(x)/p_{\text{train}}(x)\) so the training average mimics the test average (Equation 26.5 of Chapter 26). The weighted fit pays attention to the region that matters at test time.

from scipy.stats import norm
w = norm.pdf(x_tr, 2.5, 0.5) / norm.pdf(x_tr, 0.0, 0.7)       # p_test(x) / p_train(x)
c_w = np.polyfit(x_tr, y_tr, 1, w=np.sqrt(w))                  # weighted least squares
mse_w = lambda x, y: np.mean((np.polyval(c_w, x) - y) ** 2)
print(f"unweighted fit on shifted test: {mse(x_te, y_te):.3f}")
print(f"weighted fit on shifted test  : {mse_w(x_te, y_te):.3f}")
print(f"effective sample size after weighting: {w.sum() ** 2 / (w ** 2).sum():.1f} of {len(w)}")
unweighted fit on shifted test: 8.907
weighted fit on shifted test  : 3.687
effective sample size after weighting: 9.0 of 200

The weighted fit is better on the test region, but the effective sample size has collapsed: few training points carry the weight. Importance weighting cannot create information where the training data has none. When the supports barely overlap, it fails.

27.6 Production

scikit-learn provides cross-validation and learning curves.

from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score, KFold
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import PolynomialFeatures
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression

best = {}
for d in [1, 3, 9]:
    model = make_pipeline(PolynomialFeatures(d), LinearRegression())
    scores = cross_val_score(model, (2 * x - 1).reshape(-1, 1), y,
                             cv=KFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0),
                             scoring="neg_mean_squared_error")
    best[d] = -scores.mean()
print({d: round(v, 3) for d, v in best.items()})
{1: np.float64(0.245), 3: np.float64(0.085), 9: np.float64(0.383)}

27.7 Failure modes

Test-set reuse. Repeated evaluation and tuning on one test set gives an optimistic score (Figure 27.2).

Leakage. Duplicates, preprocessing fitted on the full data, or future information in a time series inflate scores (Chapter 26).

Shift not noticed. A model that validates well can fail in deployment. Monitor input statistics and performance on fresh labelled samples.

Random splits on dependent data. Frames of one video, patients, or documents from one source must stay on one side of the split. Use grouped or chronological splits.

Metric mismatch. A model can generalise in terms of the loss and fail in terms of the metric that matters (Chapter 14).

Benchmark saturation and contamination. Public test sets leak into web-scale training data. High scores may reflect memorisation.

27.9 System connection

NoteWhat the system gained in this chapter

The system can now separate what it fits from what it will do. It has the expected and empirical risks, an explanation of why selecting a model biases its training score, honest ways to estimate the risk (validation, cross-validation, a single-use test set), and names for the failures that remain: leakage and shift.

What controls the size of the gap? Flexibility is one lever. Chapter 28 shows how to trade it off with bias and variance and how to restrain it with regularisation.

Chapter summary

  • Expected risk \(R(f)\) is what we want. Empirical risk \(\hat R_N(f)\) is what we measure.
  • For a fixed \(f\), \(\hat R_N\) is unbiased. For the minimiser, it is biased low (optimiser’s curse).
  • More flexible models reduce training error always, and test error only up to a point.
  • Validation, cross-validation, and a single-use test set estimate \(R\) honestly.
  • Shifts in \(p(x)\), \(p(y)\), or \(p(y\mid x)\) break the guarantee. Importance weighting helps only where supports overlap.
