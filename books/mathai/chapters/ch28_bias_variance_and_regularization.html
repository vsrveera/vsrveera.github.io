28  Bias, Variance and Regularization

NoteKey ideas
  • The expected squared error splits into bias², variance, and irreducible noise.
  • Simple models have high bias and low variance. Flexible models have the opposite. The best model balances them.
  • Regularisation restrains a flexible model. Ridge shrinks weights smoothly. Lasso sets some exactly to zero.
  • Dropout injects noise and keeps the expected activation fixed. Early stopping limits how far training goes.
  • Regularisation lowers the test loss even when test accuracy does not change.

28.1 The system problem

Chapter 27 showed that a flexible model fits the training data better and the test data worse. We need to understand why the test error rises, and what to do about it.

How do we trade fitting the data against fitting the noise?

28.2 The bias–variance decomposition

Let the data come from \(y=f(x)+\varepsilon\), with noise \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon]=0\) and \(\text{Var}(\varepsilon)=\sigma^2\). A model \(\hat f\) is fitted to a random training set, so \(\hat f(x)\) is random too. At a fixed input \(x\):

\[ \mathbb{E}\bigl[(\hat f(x)-y)^{2}\bigr]=\underbrace{\bigl(\mathbb{E}[\hat f(x)]-f(x)\bigr)^2}_{\text{bias}^2}+\underbrace{\text{Var}\bigl(\hat f(x)\bigr)}_{\text{variance}}+\underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{noise}}. \tag{28.1}\]

TipDerivation

Write \(\bar f=\mathbb{E}[\hat f(x)]\). The new noise \(\varepsilon\) is independent of \(\hat f\). Then \[ \mathbb{E}[(\hat f-f-\varepsilon)^2]=\mathbb{E}[(\hat f-f)^2]-2\,\mathbb{E}[\hat f-f]\,\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon]+\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^2]=\mathbb{E}[(\hat f-f)^2]+\sigma^2. \] For the first term, add and subtract \(\bar f\) (the same step as Equation 26.3 of Chapter 26): \[ \mathbb{E}[(\hat f-f)^2]=\mathbb{E}[(\hat f-\bar f)^2]+(\bar f-f)^2=\text{Var}(\hat f)+\text{bias}^2.\ \blacksquare \]

Term Meaning Falls when
Bias² the model class cannot represent the truth the model is more flexible
Variance the fit changes a lot between training sets the model is simpler, or there is more data
Noise \(\sigma^2\) randomness in \(y\) itself never: it is a floor

We measure the three terms by simulation. We draw 300 independent training sets of 25 points, fit polynomials of each degree, and compute the bias and variance of the fitted curve across the sets.

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
f_true = lambda t: np.sin(2 * np.pi * t)
sigma, n, K = 0.3, 25, 300
x_eval = np.linspace(0.02, 0.98, 100)
degrees = np.arange(0, 11)
bias2, var, direct = [], [], []
for d in degrees:
    preds = np.zeros((K, len(x_eval)))
    for k in range(K):
        x = rng.uniform(0, 1, n); y = f_true(x) + rng.normal(0, sigma, n)
        c = np.polynomial.polynomial.polyfit(2 * x - 1, y, d)
        preds[k] = np.polynomial.polynomial.polyval(2 * x_eval - 1, c)
    mean_pred = preds.mean(axis=0)
    bias2.append(np.mean((mean_pred - f_true(x_eval)) ** 2))
    var.append(np.mean(preds.var(axis=0)))
    y_new = f_true(x_eval) + rng.normal(0, sigma, (K, len(x_eval)))        # fresh noisy targets
    direct.append(np.mean((preds - y_new) ** 2))
bias2, var, direct = map(np.array, (bias2, var, direct))
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.4, 3.7))
ax.semilogy(degrees, bias2, "o-", color=PALETTE["data"], label="bias²")
ax.semilogy(degrees, var, "o-", color=PALETTE["loss"], label="variance")
ax.semilogy(degrees, bias2 + var + sigma ** 2, "-", color=PALETTE["model"], lw=2, label="bias² + variance + σ²")
ax.semilogy(degrees, direct, "ko", ms=4, label="test error (direct)")
ax.axhline(sigma ** 2, ls="--", color=PALETTE["neutral"]); ax.set_xlabel("polynomial degree")
ax.legend(fontsize=8)
plt.show()
print("degree with lowest test error:", int(degrees[np.argmin(direct)]))
print("max |decomposition − direct| / direct:", np.max(np.abs(bias2 + var + sigma ** 2 - direct) / direct).round(3))
degree with lowest test error: 3
max |decomposition − direct| / direct: 0.013

A model complex enough to represent the truth (low bias) but too free to be pinned down by 25 points (high variance) does badly. A regulariser gives up a little bias for a large drop in variance.

28.3 Ridge regression: smooth shrinkage

Ridge (L2) adds \(\lambda\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2\) to the squared error (a Gaussian prior, Chapter 25). To see what it does, use the SVD of the design matrix, \(\boldsymbol{\Phi}=\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{\intercal}\) (Chapter 7).

TipDerivation: ridge shrinks along singular directions

Least squares gives \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}=\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{U}^{\intercal}\mathbf{y}\). The ridge solution is \((\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\Phi}+\lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\intercal}\mathbf{y}\). Substituting the SVD and using \(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\Phi}=\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}\mathbf{V}^{\intercal}\): \[ \hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\text{ridge}}=\mathbf{V}\,\text{diag}\!\Bigl(\frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_i^2+\lambda}\Bigr)\mathbf{U}^{\intercal}\mathbf{y} =\sum_{i}\underbrace{\frac{\sigma_i^2}{\sigma_i^2+\lambda}}_{\text{filter factor}}\;\frac{\mathbf{u}_i^{\intercal}\mathbf{y}}{\sigma_i}\,\mathbf{v}_i. \] Each term of the least-squares solution is multiplied by a factor in \((0,1)\). Directions with a large singular value (\(\sigma_i^{2}\gg\lambda\)) are kept. Directions with a small one (\(\sigma_i^{2}\ll\lambda\)), which are exactly those that amplify noise, are suppressed. \(\blacksquare\)

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
xt = np.sort(rng.uniform(0, 1, 25))
Phi = np.vander(2 * xt - 1, 10, increasing=True)
U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(Phi, full_matrices=False)
fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9.5, 3.3))
ax1.semilogy(range(1, 11), s, "o-", color=PALETTE["data"])
ax1.set_xlabel("index i"); ax1.set_ylabel("singular value σᵢ")
for lam, c in zip([1e-4, 1e-2, 1.0], [PALETTE["grad"], PALETTE["model"], PALETTE["loss"]]):
    ax2.semilogx(s, s ** 2 / (s ** 2 + lam), "o-", color=c, label=f"λ = {lam:g}")
ax2.set_xlabel("singular value σᵢ"); ax2.set_ylabel("filter factor"); ax2.legend()
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

y_obs = f_true(xt) + rng.normal(0, 0.3, 25); lam = 1e-2
w_direct = np.linalg.solve(Phi.T @ Phi + lam * np.eye(10), Phi.T @ y_obs)
w_svd = Vt.T @ ((s / (s ** 2 + lam)) * (U.T @ y_obs))
print("SVD form equals the closed form:", np.allclose(w_direct, w_svd))
SVD form equals the closed form: True

28.4 L1 versus L2: why lasso gives zeros

Both penalties can be written as constraints. Minimise the loss subject to \(\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert_{p}\le t\). The loss has elliptical contours around the unconstrained optimum \(\mathbf{w}^*\). The constrained optimum is where the lowest contour touches the constraint set.

  • The L2 set is a disc. It is smooth, so the contact point is generic: both coordinates are non-zero.
  • The L1 set is a diamond with corners on the axes. A growing ellipse tends to touch a corner first, where one coordinate is exactly zero.
ang = 0.6
R = np.array([[np.cos(ang), -np.sin(ang)], [np.sin(ang), np.cos(ang)]])
A = R @ np.diag([1.0, 8.0]) @ R.T
w_star = np.array([2.0, 0.3])
loss = lambda W: np.einsum("...i,ij,...j->...", W - w_star, A, W - w_star)
th = np.linspace(0, 2 * np.pi, 40000)
circ = np.stack([np.cos(th), np.sin(th)], 1)
diam = circ / np.abs(circ).sum(1, keepdims=True)
sol_l2 = circ[np.argmin(loss(circ))]; sol_l1 = diam[np.argmin(loss(diam))]

gx, gy = np.meshgrid(np.linspace(-0.5, 2.6, 300), np.linspace(-1.2, 1.4, 300))
Z = loss(np.stack([gx, gy], -1))
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9.5, 3.9))
for ax, shape, sol, title in zip(axes, [circ, diam], [sol_l2, sol_l1], ["L2 constraint", "L1 constraint"]):
    ax.contour(gx, gy, Z, levels=10, cmap="viridis", alpha=0.7)
    ax.fill(*shape.T, color=PALETTE["model"], alpha=0.25); ax.plot(*shape.T, color=PALETTE["model"])
    ax.plot(*w_star, "*", color="black", ms=14); ax.plot(*sol, "o", color=PALETTE["loss"], ms=9)
    ax.set_aspect("equal"); ax.set_title(f"{title}: solution ({sol[0]:.2f}, {sol[1]:.2f})")
    ax.axhline(0, c="#9CA3AF", lw=0.6); ax.axvline(0, c="#9CA3AF", lw=0.6)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

In high dimension the diamond has many corners and edges on the axes. L1 regularisation therefore selects a sparse set of features (Chapter 25).

28.5 Dropout

Dropout zeros each hidden unit independently with probability \(p\) during training. The surviving values are scaled by \(1/(1-p)\) (“inverted dropout”):

\[ \tilde{h}_i=\frac{m_i}{1-p}\,h_i,\qquad m_{i}\sim\text{Bernoulli}(1-p). \tag{28.2}\]

The scale keeps the expected value fixed: \(\mathbb{E}[\tilde h_i]=\frac{1-p}{1-p}h_i=h_i\). At test time the mask is removed and nothing needs rescaling.

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
h = np.array([1.0, -2.0, 0.5, 3.0]); p = 0.5
masks = rng.random((200_000, 4)) > p
h_drop = masks * h / (1 - p)
print("E[dropout(h)] ≈", np.round(h_drop.mean(axis=0), 3), "  h =", h)
print("variance of dropout(h):", np.round(h_drop.var(axis=0), 2),
      "  formula h² p/(1−p):", np.round(h ** 2 * p / (1 - p), 2))
E[dropout(h)] ≈ [ 1.001 -2.     0.499  3.   ]   h = [ 1.  -2.   0.5  3. ]
variance of dropout(h): [1.   4.   0.25 9.  ]   formula h² p/(1−p): [1.   4.   0.25 9.  ]

Dropout makes the network robust to the loss of any unit: no unit can rely on a specific partner. It acts like training an exponentially large ensemble of thinned networks that share weights.

28.5.1 A small-data experiment

We train a wide network on only 150 digit images, a setting where overfitting is easy. We compare no regularisation, dropout (\(p=0.5\)), weight decay, and both. Optimiser: AdamW (Chapter 19).

import torch, torch.nn as nn
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
D = load_digits()
Xtr, Xte, ytr, yte = train_test_split(D.data / 16.0, D.target, train_size=150,
                                      random_state=0, stratify=D.target)
Xt, yt = torch.tensor(Xtr, dtype=torch.float32), torch.tensor(ytr)
Xv, yv = torch.tensor(Xte, dtype=torch.float32), torch.tensor(yte)

def run(p=0.0, wd=0.0, steps=400):
    torch.manual_seed(0)
    m = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(64, 256), nn.ReLU(), nn.Dropout(p), nn.Linear(256, 10))
    opt = torch.optim.AdamW(m.parameters(), lr=0.003, weight_decay=wd)
    val = []
    for _ in range(steps):
        m.train(); opt.zero_grad()
        nn.functional.cross_entropy(m(Xt), yt).backward(); opt.step()
        m.eval()
        with torch.no_grad():
            val.append(nn.functional.cross_entropy(m(Xv), yv).item())
    with torch.no_grad():
        acc = (m(Xv).argmax(1) == yv).float().mean().item()
    return acc, np.array(val)

configs = {"none": {}, "dropout 0.5": {"p": 0.5}, "weight decay 0.5": {"wd": 0.5},
           "both": {"p": 0.5, "wd": 0.5}}
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.4, 3.6))
print(f"{'regulariser':18s} {'test acc':>9s} {'min val loss':>13s} {'at step':>8s} {'final val loss':>15s}")
for (name, kw), c in zip(configs.items(), [PALETTE["data"], PALETTE["model"], PALETTE["grad"], PALETTE["loss"]]):
    acc, val = run(**kw)
    ax.plot(val, color=c, label=name); ax.plot(val.argmin(), val.min(), "o", color=c)
    print(f"{name:18s} {acc:9.3f} {val.min():13.3f} {int(val.argmin()):8d} {val[-1]:15.3f}")
ax.set_xlabel("step"); ax.set_ylabel("validation loss"); ax.legend(fontsize=8)
plt.show()
regulariser         test acc  min val loss  at step  final val loss
none                   0.918         0.349       84           0.402
dropout 0.5            0.919         0.315      161           0.353
weight decay 0.5       0.918         0.323      274           0.324
both                   0.919         0.290      357           0.297

On this easy problem, test accuracy is the same for all four. What differs is the loss: the regularised networks are less over-confident, and their validation loss does not climb. If the loss feeds a decision (a probability threshold, a ranking by confidence), the difference matters.

28.5.2 Early stopping

The unregularised curve above shows another remedy. The validation loss has a minimum, then rises while the training loss keeps falling. Early stopping keeps the weights from the step of the minimum. It costs nothing and acts as an implicit regulariser: for a quadratic loss, stopping gradient descent early has an effect close to a ridge penalty, with \(\lambda\sim1/(\eta t)\).

28.6 Other restraints

  • Data augmentation. Add transformed copies of the data (shifts, crops, flips). It builds a prior (invariance) into the training set. It is often the most effective regulariser for vision.
  • Label smoothing (Chapter 24). Limits confidence.
  • Weight sharing and architecture. A convolutional layer has far fewer parameters than a dense layer (Chapter 12): a hard constraint on flexibility.
  • More data. The variance falls as \(1/N\).
  • Ensembling. Averaging models reduces variance without raising bias.

28.7 An observation: the textbook curve is not the whole story

The U-shaped test curve describes classical models well. Modern networks have far more parameters than training points and still generalise. Some settings show a double descent: test error peaks near the point where the model can just interpolate the data, then falls again as capacity grows further. We do not reproduce it here. It is sensitive to the setup, and the theory is still developing. The practical conclusion is that parameter count alone is a poor measure of flexibility for overparameterised models (Chapter 29).

28.8 Production

All the restraints are one-liners in PyTorch and scikit-learn. We check ridge against scikit-learn and the dropout scale in PyTorch.

from sklearn.linear_model import Ridge
w_skl = Ridge(alpha=lam, fit_intercept=False).fit(Phi, y_obs).coef_
print("ridge: our SVD form equals sklearn Ridge:", np.allclose(w_svd, w_skl, atol=1e-6))

28.9 Failure modes

Regularising biases and normalisation scales. Weight decay is normally applied only to weight matrices.

Over-regularising. A very large \(\lambda\) makes the model constant: high bias. Choose \(\lambda\) on a validation set, on a log scale.

Dropout with normalisation. Dropout changes the variance of activations between training and test. In networks with batch normalisation, this mismatch can hurt. Many modern Transformers use little or no dropout during large-scale pre-training, where data is plentiful.

Dropout at inference. Forgetting to switch to eval mode gives random predictions. (Used on purpose, it gives uncertainty estimates.)

Tuning on the test set. Choose every regularisation setting on validation data (Chapter 27).

Mistaking accuracy for the whole story. As seen above, regularisation can leave accuracy unchanged and still improve the loss and calibration (Chapter 24).

28.11 System connection

NoteWhat the system gained in this chapter

The system can now control the gap between training and test performance. It understands why the gap exists (bias and variance), and has several levers: weight penalties (ridge, lasso), noise injection (dropout), stopping rules, and data and architecture constraints. These are the practical consequences of the Bayesian view of Chapter 25.

One question remains. Can we prove that a model will generalise, and say how much data we need? Chapter 29 gives the classical answer and its limits.

Chapter summary

  • \(\mathbb{E}[(\hat f-y)^2]=\text{bias}^2+\text{variance}+\sigma^2\).
  • Flexible models reduce bias and raise variance. The best test error is at an intermediate flexibility.
  • Ridge multiplies each singular direction by \(\sigma_i^2/(\sigma_i^2+\lambda)\). L1 gives sparse solutions through its corners.
  • Inverted dropout keeps \(\mathbb{E}[\tilde h]=h\). Turn it off at test time.
  • Early stopping, augmentation, label smoothing, and architecture are other restraints.
  • Regularisation may leave accuracy unchanged and improve the loss.
