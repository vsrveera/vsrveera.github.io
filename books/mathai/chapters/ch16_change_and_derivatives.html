16  Change and Derivatives

NoteKey ideas
  • The derivative \(f'(x)\) is the slope of \(f\) at \(x\): the rate of change for a tiny step.
  • Near a point, a smooth function is almost a straight line: \(f(x+h)\approx f(x)+f'(x)\,h\).
  • A partial derivative measures the effect of one input when all others are held fixed.
  • The derivative of the loss with respect to a parameter is its sensitivity. It tells the model which way to move.
  • A numerical derivative has two errors that pull in opposite directions. The step \(h\) has an optimum.

16.1 The system problem

Part IV gave us a number, the loss, that says how wrong the model is. Part V asks how the model changes itself to reduce it.

If we change one parameter by a tiny amount, how much does the loss change?

The answer is a derivative. This chapter builds it from the slope of a curve. Chapter 17 handles many parameters at once. Chapter 18 computes all of them fast.

16.2 Intuition: slope at a point

A car’s position is a function of time. Its speed at one moment is the rate at which position changes at that moment. We cannot measure it from a single instant. We measure the distance over a short time interval and shrink the interval.

The same idea applies to any function. The secant through \((x,f(x))\) and \((x+h,f(x+h))\) has slope \(\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\). As \(h\to0\) the secant turns into the tangent.

f = lambda x: x ** 2
x0 = 1.0
xs = np.linspace(-0.2, 2.6, 200)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(5.4, 3.8))
ax.plot(xs, f(xs), color="black", lw=2)
for h, col in zip([1.0, 0.5, 0.1], [PALETTE["loss"], PALETTE["model"], PALETTE["grad"]]):
    slope = (f(x0 + h) - f(x0)) / h
    ax.plot(xs, f(x0) + slope * (xs - x0), "--", color=col, label=f"h = {h}: slope {slope:.2f}")
    ax.plot([x0 + h], [f(x0 + h)], "o", color=col)
ax.plot(xs, f(x0) + 2 * (xs - x0), color=PALETTE["data"], lw=2, label="tangent: slope 2")
ax.plot([x0], [f(x0)], "ko"); ax.set_ylim(-0.5, 5); ax.legend(fontsize=8)
plt.show()

16.3 The mathematical object

The derivative of \(f\) at \(x\) is the limit of the secant slope:

\[ f'(x)=\frac{df}{dx}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}. \tag{16.1}\]

A function is differentiable at \(x\) if the limit exists. Then, for small \(h\),

\[ f(x+h)\approx f(x)+f'(x)\,h. \tag{16.2}\]

This is the most important use of the derivative. It replaces a curve by a straight line that is accurate close to \(x\). We study how accurate it is below.

16.3.1 Rules

Derivatives of combinations follow from a few rules.

Rule Formula
Constant multiple \((c\,f)'=c\,f'\)
Sum \((f+g)'=f'+g'\)
Product \((f\,g)'=f'g+f\,g'\)
Quotient \((f/g)'=(f'g-f\,g')/g^2\)
Chain (composition) \((g\circ f)'(x)=g'(f(x))\,f'(x)\)

The chain rule is the engine of backpropagation. Chapter 18 develops it in full.

16.3.2 Common derivatives

\(f(x)\) \(f'(x)\) Note
\(x^n\) \(n\,x^{n-1}\) powers
\(e^x\) \(e^x\) its own slope
\(\ln x\) \(1/x\) \(x>0\)
\(\sigma(x)=\frac1{1+e^{-x}}\) \(\sigma(x)(1-\sigma(x))\) sigmoid
\(\tanh x\) \(1-\tanh^2x\)
\(\max(0,x)\) \(1\) for \(x>0\), \(0\) for \(x<0\) not defined at \(0\)
TipDerivation: the derivative of \(x^n\) and of the sigmoid

Power. By the binomial expansion, \((x+h)^n=x^n+n\,x^{n-1}h+O(h^2)\). So \(\frac{(x+h)^n-x^n}{h}=n\,x^{n-1}+O(h)\to n\,x^{n-1}\).

Sigmoid. Write \(\sigma(x)=(1+e^{-x})^{-1}\). By the chain rule with \(u=1+e^{-x}\): \[ \sigma'(x)=-u^{-2}\cdot(-e^{-x})=\frac{e^{-x}}{(1+e^{-x})^2}=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}\cdot\frac{e^{-x}}{1+e^{-x}}=\sigma(x)\bigl(1-\sigma(x)\bigr).\ \blacksquare \] The maximum is \(\sigma'(0)=\frac14\).

16.4 Partial derivatives and sensitivity

A loss depends on many parameters, \(L(\theta_1,\dots,\theta_n)\). The partial derivative with respect to \(\theta_i\) treats all other parameters as constants:

\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta_i}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{L(\ldots,\theta_i+h,\ldots)-L(\ldots,\theta_i,\ldots)}{h}. \tag{16.3}\]

It is the sensitivity of the loss to parameter \(\theta_i\): how fast \(L\) changes if we nudge only \(\theta_i\). A large magnitude means the parameter matters at this point. The sign says which way \(L\) increases.

We compute the sensitivity of the Chapter 1 line-fit loss with respect to both parameters, at the starting point \((a,b)=(0,0)\).

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = rng.uniform(-2, 2, 30)
y = 2.0 * x + 1.0 + rng.normal(0, 0.5, 30)

def L(a, b):
    return np.mean((a * x + b - y) ** 2)

a0, b0, h = 0.0, 0.0, 1e-5
dL_da = (L(a0 + h, b0) - L(a0 - h, b0)) / (2 * h)
dL_db = (L(a0, b0 + h) - L(a0, b0 - h)) / (2 * h)
print(f"at (a, b) = (0, 0):  ∂L/∂a = {dL_da:+.3f}   ∂L/∂b = {dL_db:+.3f}")
print("analytic (Chapter 1):",
      f"∂L/∂a = {2 * np.mean((a0 * x + b0 - y) * x):+.3f},",
      f"∂L/∂b = {2 * np.mean(a0 * x + b0 - y):+.3f}")
at (a, b) = (0, 0):  ∂L/∂a = -6.803   ∂L/∂b = -2.826
analytic (Chapter 1): ∂L/∂a = -6.803, ∂L/∂b = -2.826

Both partials are negative: increasing \(a\) or \(b\) lowers the loss here. The model should move in the positive direction in both. The partials contain what the learning rule of Chapter 1 needs.

16.5 Local linear approximation

How good is Equation 16.2? By Taylor’s theorem, the error is of second order in \(h\):

\[ f(x+h)=f(x)+f'(x)h+\tfrac12f''(x)h^2+O(h^3). \tag{16.4}\]

So the linear approximation has error \(\approx\frac12|f''|\,h^2\). Halving the step cuts the error by four. A large \(|f''|\) means the function curves quickly and the line is only good for very small steps.

hs = np.logspace(-4, 0, 30)
err = np.abs(np.exp(1 + hs) - (np.exp(1) + np.exp(1) * hs))
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(5.6, 3.4))
ax.loglog(hs, err, "o-", color=PALETTE["loss"], label="|f(x+h) − linear approx|")
ax.loglog(hs, 0.5 * np.exp(1) * hs ** 2, "--", color=PALETTE["neutral"], label="½ f″ h²")
ax.set_xlabel("step size h"); ax.set_ylabel("error"); ax.legend()
plt.show()

This is the reason that a learning rate must be small: the update \(\theta'=\theta-\eta\nabla L\) trusts the linear approximation (Chapter 1). If \(\eta\) is large, the approximation fails.

16.6 Numerical differentiation

We can estimate a derivative without any formula. Two common estimates are:

\[ D_h^{\text{fwd}}f=\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h},\qquad D_h^{\text{ctr}}f=\frac{f(x+h)-f(x-h)}{2h}. \tag{16.5}\]

TipDerivation: errors of the two estimates

From the Taylor series, \(f(x\pm h)=f\pm f'h+\tfrac12f''h^{2}\pm\tfrac16f'''h^3+\dots\).

  • Forward: \(D_h^{\text{fwd}}f=f'+\tfrac12f''h+\dots\). The truncation error is \(O(h)\).
  • Central: subtracting the two series cancels the even terms: \(D_h^{\text{ctr}}f=f'+\tfrac16f'''h^2+\dots\). The error is \(O(h^2)\).

Computers add a second error. Numbers have about \(\varepsilon\approx10^{-16}\) relative precision. The subtraction \(f(x+h)-f(x)\) loses digits when \(h\) is tiny, and dividing by \(h\) then magnifies the loss. The rounding error is about \(\varepsilon\,|f|/h\).

Truncation error falls with \(h\). Rounding error rises as \(h\) falls. The total has a minimum.

f = df = np.exp
points = np.linspace(0.5, 2.0, 40)           # average over many x to smooth luck
hs = np.logspace(-14, 0, 200)
X0 = points[:, None]
fwd = np.mean(np.abs((f(X0 + hs) - f(X0)) / hs - df(X0)) / df(X0), axis=0)
ctr = np.mean(np.abs((f(X0 + hs) - f(X0 - hs)) / (2 * hs) - df(X0)) / df(X0), axis=0)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.2, 3.6))
ax.loglog(hs, fwd, color=PALETTE["loss"], label="forward difference")
ax.loglog(hs, ctr, color=PALETTE["grad"], label="central difference")
ax.set_xlabel("step size h"); ax.set_ylabel("relative error"); ax.legend()
plt.show()
print(f"forward: best h = {hs[np.argmin(fwd)]:.1e}, error {fwd.min():.1e}")
print(f"central: best h = {hs[np.argmin(ctr)]:.1e}, error {ctr.min():.1e}")
forward: best h = 1.3e-08, error 4.9e-09
central: best h = 4.5e-06, error 8.5e-12

Balancing the two errors gives \(h^*\approx\sqrt{\varepsilon}\approx10^{-8}\) for the forward difference and \(h^*\approx\varepsilon^{1/3}\approx10^{-5}\) for the central difference. This is why the gradient checks in Chapters 1 and 18 used \(h=10^{-5}\) with central differences.

16.7 Where the derivative does not exist

Some functions used in AI have kinks. \(|x|\) and \(\text{ReLU}\) are not differentiable at 0. The left and right slopes differ.

def one_sided(f, x0, h=1e-6):
    return (f(x0) - f(x0 - h)) / h, (f(x0 + h) - f(x0)) / h

relu = lambda x: np.maximum(0, x)
for name, fn in [("|x|", np.abs), ("ReLU", relu)]:
    left, right = one_sided(fn, 0.0)
    ctr = (fn(1e-6) - fn(-1e-6)) / 2e-6
    print(f"{name:5s} left slope {left:+.1f}, right slope {right:+.1f}, "
          f"central difference {ctr:+.2f}")
|x|   left slope -1.0, right slope +1.0, central difference +0.00
ReLU  left slope +0.0, right slope +1.0, central difference +0.50

The central difference reports a plausible-looking number (\(0\) or \(0.5\)) that is not a derivative. Frameworks pick a convention for the point itself, usually 0 for ReLU. The point is hit with probability zero in practice, and any value in \([0,1]\) is a valid subgradient.

16.8 Production: derivatives by machine

PyTorch computes derivatives exactly, not by finite differences. We compare for one function.

import torch

def g(x):
    return torch.sigmoid(3 * x) * torch.exp(-x ** 2)

xt = torch.tensor(0.7, dtype=torch.float64, requires_grad=True)
g(xt).backward()
auto = xt.grad.item()

h = 1e-5
x_val = 0.7
num = (g(torch.tensor(x_val + h, dtype=torch.float64)).item()
       - g(torch.tensor(x_val - h, dtype=torch.float64)).item()) / (2 * h)
print(f"autograd: {auto:.10f}   central difference: {num:.10f}")
print(f"difference: {abs(auto - num):.1e}")
autograd: -0.5854749982   central difference: -0.5854749981
difference: 1.0e-10

Automatic differentiation applies the chain rule to the exact operations. It has no step size and no truncation error. The finite difference remains the right tool for testing.

16.9 Failure modes

Cancellation. For tiny \(h\), \(f(x+h)-f(x)\) subtracts two nearly equal numbers and keeps few correct digits. This is the rising branch in Figure 16.4.

Wrong step. A step that is far above or below the optimum gives a poor estimate. For functions whose scale is not near 1, scale \(h\) with \(|x|\).

Kinks and jumps. Derivatives are undefined at kinks and zero almost everywhere for step functions (argmax, floor). A model cannot learn through them (Chapter 8).

Large curvature. Where \(|f''|\) is large, the linear approximation fails for modest steps. A learning rate that is fine in one region may diverge in another (Chapter 20).

Noisy functions. If \(f\) is a noisy measurement (a stochastic loss), a small \(h\) divides noise by \(h\) and gives garbage. Use larger steps or average.

16.11 System connection

NoteWhat the system gained in this chapter

The system knows how to ask: if I change this one parameter, what happens to the loss? It has the derivative, the linear approximation, and a way to test any derivative numerically.

A model has millions of parameters. We need all the partial derivatives together, and a sense of direction in the space of all parameters. Chapter 17 provides it.

Chapter summary

  • The derivative is the limit of the secant slope: \(f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\).
  • Locally, \(f(x+h)\approx f(x)+f'(x)h\). The error is \(O(h^2)\).
  • Sum, product, quotient, and chain rules build derivatives of combinations.
  • A partial derivative holds the other variables fixed. It is the sensitivity of the output to one input.
  • Finite differences have truncation error and rounding error. Central differences with \(h\approx10^{-5}\) are a good test.
  • Kinks have no derivative. Frameworks choose a subgradient.
  • Autodiff gives exact derivatives for the exact operations.
