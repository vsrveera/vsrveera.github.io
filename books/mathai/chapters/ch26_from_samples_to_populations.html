26  From Samples to Populations

NoteKey ideas
  • A dataset is a sample from an unknown population. Everything we compute from it is itself random.
  • An estimator is a rule that turns a sample into a number. Its quality is its bias and variance: \(\text{MSE}=\text{bias}^2+\text{variance}\).
  • The standard error of a mean falls as \(1/\sqrt{n}\). Dividing by \(n-1\) (not \(n\)) makes the variance estimate unbiased.
  • The bootstrap estimates uncertainty by resampling the data.
  • All of this assumes samples are independent and identically distributed. Leakage and correlation break it.

26.1 The system problem

A model is trained and tested on a finite set of examples. We report “92% accuracy”. But a different test set would give 91% or 94%. And the real aim is accuracy on all future data, which we never see.

What can a finite dataset tell us about the world it came from, and how sure can we be?

Part VII studies this question. This chapter builds the tools: sampling, estimators, and uncertainty of estimates.

26.2 Population and sample

The population is the distribution \(p\) that generates the data: all the cases we care about. A sample is \(N\) draws \(x_1,\dots,x_N\) from it. We assume they are independent and identically distributed (iid).

The sample defines its own distribution, the empirical distribution \(\hat p_N\), which puts mass \(1/N\) on each data point. Its CDF is the empirical CDF

\[ \hat{F}_N(t)=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{1}[x_{i}\le t]. \tag{26.1}\]

It converges to the true CDF as \(N\) grows.

from scipy.stats import norm
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
t = np.linspace(-3.5, 3.5, 600)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.2, 3.5))
ax.plot(t, norm.cdf(t), color="black", lw=2, label="true CDF")
for n, c in zip([10, 100, 1000], [PALETTE["loss"], PALETTE["model"], PALETTE["grad"]]):
    x = np.sort(rng.normal(size=n))
    F = np.searchsorted(x, t, side="right") / n
    ax.plot(t, F, color=c, lw=1.4, label=f"N = {n}   max gap {np.abs(F - norm.cdf(t)).max():.3f}")
ax.legend(fontsize=8); ax.set_xlabel("t")
plt.show()

26.3 Estimators: bias and variance

An estimator \(\hat\theta\) is a function of the sample. Because the sample is random, \(\hat\theta\) is a random variable with its own distribution, the sampling distribution. Two numbers describe how good it is:

\[ \text{Bias}(\hat\theta)=\mathbb{E}[\hat\theta]-\theta,\qquad\text{Var}(\hat\theta)=\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\hat\theta-\mathbb{E}\hat\theta)^{2}\bigr]. \tag{26.2}\]

Their combination is the mean squared error:

\[ \text{MSE}(\hat\theta)=\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\hat\theta-\theta)^{2}\bigr]=\text{Bias}^2+\text{Var}. \tag{26.3}\]

TipDerivation of the decomposition

Add and subtract \(\mathbb{E}[\hat\theta]\): \[ \mathbb{E}\bigl[(\hat\theta-\theta)^{2}\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\hat\theta-\mathbb{E}\hat\theta)^{2}\bigr]+2\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[\hat\theta-\mathbb{E}\hat\theta\bigr](\mathbb{E}\hat\theta-\theta)+(\mathbb{E}\hat\theta-\theta)^2. \] The middle term is zero because \(\mathbb{E}[\hat\theta-\mathbb{E}\hat\theta]=0\). \(\blacksquare\)

This is the same decomposition that Chapter 28 applies to the prediction error of a model.

26.3.1 The sample mean

For iid samples with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\), the sample mean \(\bar{x}=\frac1N\sum x_i\) is unbiased: \(\mathbb{E}[\bar{x}]=\mu\). Its variance is \(\sigma^2/N\) (Chapter 21). Its standard deviation is the standard error:

\[ \text{SE}(\bar{x})=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{N}}. \tag{26.4}\]

26.3.2 The variance: why \(N-1\)

The “obvious” estimator of the variance is \(\hat\sigma^2_N=\frac1N\sum_i(x_i-\bar{x})^2\). It is biased low, because \(\bar{x}\) was fitted to the same data and is, on average, closer to the points than the true \(\mu\).

TipDerivation: the bias of the variance estimate

Write \(x_i-\bar{x}=(x_i-\mu)-(\bar{x}-\mu)\). Then \[ \sum_i(x_i-\bar{x})^2=\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2-N(\bar{x}-\mu)^2. \] Take expectations: \(\mathbb{E}\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2=N\sigma^2\) and \(\mathbb{E}\,N(\bar{x}-\mu)^2=N\cdot\sigma^2/N=\sigma^2\). So \[ \mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sum_i(x_i-\bar{x})^{2}\Bigr]=(N-1)\,\sigma^2. \] Dividing by \(N-1\) gives an unbiased estimator. Dividing by \(N\) gives \(\frac{N-1}{N}\sigma^2\). \(\blacksquare\)

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
Ns = [2, 3, 5, 10, 20, 30]
biased, unbiased = [], []
for N in Ns:
    x = rng.normal(size=(100_000, N))
    ss = ((x - x.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)) ** 2).sum(axis=1)
    biased.append((ss / N).mean()); unbiased.append((ss / (N - 1)).mean())
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(5.8, 3.4))
ax.plot(Ns, biased, "o-", color=PALETTE["loss"], label="divide by N")
ax.plot(Ns, unbiased, "o-", color=PALETTE["grad"], label="divide by N − 1")
ax.plot(Ns, [(n - 1) / n for n in Ns], ":", color="black", label="(N − 1)/N")
ax.axhline(1, ls="--", color=PALETTE["neutral"]); ax.set_xlabel("sample size N"); ax.legend()
ax.set_ylabel("average estimate (truth 1)")
plt.show()

NumPy’s np.var divides by \(N\) by default (ddof=0). np.var(x, ddof=1) divides by \(N-1\). PyTorch’s torch.var divides by \(N-1\) by default. This difference causes silent bugs.

26.4 The bootstrap

The standard error formula \(\sigma/\sqrt N\) exists for the mean. For a median, a correlation, or a model’s accuracy, there is no simple formula. The bootstrap gives one for any statistic.

Idea. The sample is our best picture of the population. Treat \(\hat p_N\) as the population, and draw new samples from the data, with replacement. Compute the statistic on each. Their spread estimates the sampling variability.

  1. Repeat \(B\) times: draw \(N\) points with replacement from the data. Compute the statistic.
  2. The standard deviation of the \(B\) values is the standard error.
  3. The 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles form a 95% confidence interval.
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
data = rng.exponential(2.0, size=40)                     # skewed; true median 2 ln 2 = 1.386
B = 2000
boot = np.array([np.median(rng.choice(data, size=len(data), replace=True)) for _ in range(B)])
lo, hi = np.quantile(boot, [0.025, 0.975])
print(f"sample median {np.median(data):.3f}; bootstrap SE {boot.std():.3f}; "
      f"95% interval [{lo:.3f}, {hi:.3f}]; true median {2 * np.log(2):.3f}")
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(5.8, 3.2))
ax.hist(boot, bins=40, color=PALETTE["data"], alpha=0.7)
ax.axvline(np.median(data), color=PALETTE["loss"], lw=2)
for q in (lo, hi): ax.axvline(q, ls="--", color="black")
ax.set_xlabel("median of a resample")
plt.show()
sample median 1.822; bootstrap SE 0.391; 95% interval [0.961, 2.332]; true median 1.386

26.4.1 Does the interval cover the truth?

A 95% interval should contain the true value in about 95% of repeated experiments. We check by simulation with many fresh datasets.

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
true_median = 2 * np.log(2)
hits, trials = 0, 400
for _ in range(trials):
    d = rng.exponential(2.0, size=40)
    bm = np.array([np.median(rng.choice(d, size=40)) for _ in range(300)])
    l, h = np.quantile(bm, [0.025, 0.975])
    hits += (l <= true_median <= h)
print(f"coverage of the 95% bootstrap interval over {trials} experiments: {hits / trials:.3f}")
coverage of the 95% bootstrap interval over 400 experiments: 0.938

26.5 How sure are we about an accuracy?

A classifier is correct on \(k\) of \(n\) test examples. Treat each test example as a Bernoulli trial. The accuracy \(\hat{a}=k/n\) has standard error \(\sqrt{\hat{a}(1-\hat{a})/n}\).

from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split

D = load_digits()
Xtr, Xte, ytr, yte = train_test_split(D.data / 16.0, D.target, test_size=0.25,
                                      random_state=0, stratify=D.target)
lr = LogisticRegression(max_iter=3000).fit(Xtr, ytr)
kn = KNeighborsClassifier(3).fit(Xtr, ytr)
c_lr = (lr.predict(Xte) == yte).astype(float)            # per-example correctness
c_kn = (kn.predict(Xte) == yte).astype(float)
n = len(yte)
for name, c in [("logistic regression", c_lr), ("3-NN", c_kn)]:
    a = c.mean(); se = np.sqrt(a * (1 - a) / n)
    print(f"{name:20s} accuracy {a:.3f} ± {1.96 * se:.3f}  (95% interval, n = {n})")

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
diffs = []
for _ in range(5000):
    idx = rng.integers(0, n, n)                          # paired bootstrap
    diffs.append(c_kn[idx].mean() - c_lr[idx].mean())
lo, hi = np.quantile(diffs, [0.025, 0.975])
print(f"3-NN minus logistic: {c_kn.mean() - c_lr.mean():+.3f}, 95% interval [{lo:+.3f}, {hi:+.3f}]")
logistic regression  accuracy 0.969 ± 0.016  (95% interval, n = 450)
3-NN                 accuracy 0.987 ± 0.011  (95% interval, n = 450)
3-NN minus logistic: +0.018, 95% interval [+0.002, +0.033]

The two accuracies differ by about two percentage points, and each has an interval of about \(\pm1.5\) points. The interval for the difference, from a paired bootstrap, just excludes 0. That is weak evidence that 3-NN is better. If it had included 0, the data could not have told the models apart. With 450 test images, claims about differences of one or two points need this kind of check.

ns = np.array([50, 100, 200, 500, 1000, 5000, 20000])
half = 100 * 1.96 * np.sqrt(0.9 * 0.1 / ns)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(5.6, 3.3))
ax.loglog(ns, half, "o-", color=PALETTE["model"])
ax.set_xlabel("number of test examples"); ax.set_ylabel("95% half-width (points)")
plt.show()
print({int(k): round(float(v), 1) for k, v in zip(ns, half)})
{50: 8.3, 100: 5.9, 200: 4.2, 500: 2.6, 1000: 1.9, 5000: 0.8, 20000: 0.4}

26.6 Monte Carlo and importance sampling

Chapter 21 used the law of large numbers to estimate expectations: \(\mathbb{E}[f(X)]\approx\frac1N\sum f(x_i)\). The error is \(\sigma_f/\sqrt N\). For rare events this is wasteful. To estimate \(P(X>4)\) for \(X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) (true value \(3.2\times10^{-5}\)), a million samples give about 30 hits.

Importance sampling draws from a proposal \(q\) that makes the rare event common, and corrects with weights:

\[ \mathbb{E}_p[f(X)]=\mathbb{E}_q\!\Bigl[f(X)\frac{p(X)}{q(X)}\Bigr]. \tag{26.5}\]

from scipy.stats import norm
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
truth = norm.sf(4.0)

def plain(n):
    return np.mean(rng.normal(size=n) > 4.0)

def importance(n):
    z = rng.normal(4.0, 1.0, size=n)                         # proposal N(4, 1)
    w = norm.pdf(z) / norm.pdf(z, 4.0, 1.0)                  # p(z) / q(z)
    return np.mean((z > 4.0) * w)

n, reps = 10_000, 300
pl = np.array([plain(n) for _ in range(reps)]); im = np.array([importance(n) for _ in range(reps)])
print(f"truth {truth:.2e}")
print(f"plain MC    : mean {pl.mean():.2e}, relative std {pl.std() / truth:.2f}")
print(f"importance  : mean {im.mean():.2e}, relative std {im.std() / truth:.2f}")
truth 3.17e-05
plain MC    : mean 2.83e-05, relative std 1.76
importance  : mean 3.16e-05, relative std 0.02

Both are unbiased. Importance sampling has a far smaller variance for the same number of samples. It is used in reinforcement learning, in rare-event simulation, and for evaluating a model on data from a different distribution (Chapter 27).

26.7 Splitting data

To measure generalisation we hold out data the model never trained on. A standard split is train (fit parameters), validation (choose hyperparameters), and test (final report, used once).

For classification, a stratified split keeps the class proportions equal in each part.

The most serious error is leakage: information from the test set reaching training. The simplest case is duplicates.

from sklearn.datasets import make_classification
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
def knn_acc(Xa, ya, Xb, yb, k=1):
    return KNeighborsClassifier(k).fit(Xa, ya).score(Xb, yb)

# a noisy problem (30% label noise), where honest accuracy is modest
Xn, yn = make_classification(n_samples=1200, n_features=10, n_informative=4, n_classes=3,
                             flip_y=0.3, class_sep=1.0, random_state=0)
Xa, Xb, ya, yb = train_test_split(Xn, yn, test_size=0.4, random_state=0)

clean = knn_acc(Xa, ya, Xb, yb)
leak_idx = rng.choice(len(Xb), size=int(0.3 * len(Xb)), replace=False)       # 30% of test
Xa_leak = np.vstack([Xa, Xb[leak_idx]]); ya_leak = np.r_[ya, yb[leak_idx]]
leaked = knn_acc(Xa_leak, ya_leak, Xb, yb)
print(f"1-NN test accuracy, clean split          : {clean:.3f}")
print(f"1-NN test accuracy, 30% of test in train : {leaked:.3f}")
1-NN test accuracy, clean split          : 0.562
1-NN test accuracy, 30% of test in train : 0.681

The leaked score is inflated. The model has not become better. It has seen the answers.

26.8 Production

SciPy provides a bootstrap. We compare it with ours.

from scipy.stats import bootstrap
res = bootstrap((data,), np.median, n_resamples=2000, confidence_level=0.95,
                method="percentile", random_state=0)
ours_lo, ours_hi = np.quantile(boot, [0.025, 0.975])
print(f"scipy bootstrap interval for the median: "
      f"[{res.confidence_interval.low:.3f}, {res.confidence_interval.high:.3f}]")
print(f"our bootstrap interval                  : [{ours_lo:.3f}, {ours_hi:.3f}]")
scipy bootstrap interval for the median: [0.961, 2.362]
our bootstrap interval                  : [0.961, 2.332]

26.9 Failure modes

Samples that are not independent. Time series, video frames, patches from one image, and several photos of one patient are correlated. Their effective sample size is far below \(N\). For an AR(1) series with correlation \(\rho\), the variance of the mean is inflated by about \(\frac{1+\rho}{1-\rho}\).

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
rho, N, reps = 0.9, 500, 2000
means, naive_se = [], []
for _ in range(reps):
    e = rng.normal(size=N); x = np.zeros(N)
    for t in range(1, N):
        x[t] = rho * x[t - 1] + e[t]
    means.append(x.mean()); naive_se.append(x.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(N))
print(f"naive standard error      : {np.mean(naive_se):.3f}")
print(f"true spread of the mean   : {np.std(means):.3f}")
print(f"effective sample size ≈ N(1−ρ)/(1+ρ) = {N * (1 - rho) / (1 + rho):.0f} of {N}")
naive standard error      : 0.100
true spread of the mean   : 0.443
effective sample size ≈ N(1−ρ)/(1+ρ) = 26 of 500

Leakage. Duplicates, near-duplicates, preprocessing fitted on all data before splitting, and future information in time series all inflate test scores.

Tiny test sets. An accuracy from 50 examples has an interval of about \(\pm8\) points.

Distribution shift. The population at deployment may differ from the one sampled (Chapter 27).

Selection. Choosing the best of many models on the test set biases the estimate upward (Chapter 27).

Heavy tails. The standard error formula assumes a finite variance. The sample mean of a Cauchy variable has no useful standard error (Chapter 21).

26.11 System connection

NoteWhat the system gained in this chapter

The system can now quantify how much a finite sample can be trusted: the bias and variance of an estimator, the standard error, bootstrap intervals, and the importance of independent, leak-free splits.

We know how well we can measure performance on held-out data. Chapter 27 asks the next question: why should performance on the data a model was trained on tell us anything about performance on new data, and when does it fail?

Chapter summary

  • A sample is \(N\) iid draws from a population. The empirical distribution converges to it.
  • \(\text{MSE}=\text{Bias}^2+\text{Var}\). The sample mean is unbiased with \(\text{SE}=\sigma/\sqrt N\).
  • The variance estimator with \(1/N\) is biased by the factor \((N-1)/N\); use \(1/(N-1)\).
  • The bootstrap resamples the data to estimate the uncertainty of any statistic.
  • An accuracy has \(\text{SE}=\sqrt{\hat{a}(1-\hat{a})/n}\). Small test sets give wide intervals.
  • Importance sampling reduces the variance for rare events.
  • Dependence and leakage invalidate all of the above.
