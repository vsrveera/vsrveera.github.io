41  Diffusion Models

NoteKey ideas
  • A diffusion model defines a fixed forward process that gradually adds Gaussian noise to data, and learns the reverse process that removes it, one small step at a time.
  • Composing Gaussian steps gives a closed form: \(\mathbf{x}_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\).
  • The reverse step is a Gaussian whose mean depends on the noise. The network is trained to predict the noise: a plain squared-error regression.
  • The predicted noise is the score of the noisy data distribution, up to a scale.
  • Sampling is sequential. DDIM takes fewer, deterministic steps.

41.1 The system problem

The VAE (Chapter 40) generates an image in one step, and the result is blurry. A single step must undo all of the complexity at once. An easier problem is to undo a tiny amount of corruption.

How do we learn to turn pure noise into data, one small denoising step at a time?

41.3 The forward process

At each step, shrink the signal a little and add Gaussian noise:

\[ q(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t-1})=\mathcal{N}\bigl(\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\ \beta_{t}\mathbf{I}\bigr),\qquad t=1,\dots,T, \tag{41.1}\]

with small noise levels \(\beta_t\). Write \(\alpha_t=1-\beta_t\) and \(\bar\alpha_t=\prod_{s\le t}\alpha_s\).

TipDerivation: the closed form

One step is \(\mathbf{x}_t=\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-1}+\sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t\) with \(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})\) (Chapter 23). Substitute the previous step: \[ \mathbf{x}_t=\sqrt{\alpha_{t}\alpha_{t-1}}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-2}+\sqrt{\alpha_t(1-\alpha_{t-1})}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t-1}+\sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t. \] The two noise terms are independent Gaussians. Their sum is Gaussian with variance (variances add, Chapter 21) \(\alpha_t(1-\alpha_{t-1})+(1-\alpha_t)=1-\alpha_{t}\alpha_{t-1}\). So the sum equals \(\sqrt{1-\alpha_{t}\alpha_{t-1}}\,\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\) for a single standard normal \(\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\). Repeating the argument down to \(\mathbf{x}_0\): \[ \boxed{\;q(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_0)=\mathcal{N}\bigl(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0,\ (1-\bar\alpha_t)\mathbf{I}\bigr),\qquad \mathbf{x}_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\;}\ \blacksquare \]

This is the key convenience: any noise level can be sampled directly, without running the chain. We check it by simulating the chain step by step.

T = 100
betas = np.linspace(1e-3, 0.12, T)
alphas = 1 - betas
abar = np.cumprod(alphas)

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x0 = 1.7                                                          # a scalar "data point"
x = np.full(200_000, x0)
for t in range(40):                                               # run 40 noising steps
    x = np.sqrt(alphas[t]) * x + np.sqrt(betas[t]) * rng.normal(size=x.shape)
print(f"after 40 steps (chain):   mean {x.mean():.3f}, std {x.std():.3f}")
print(f"closed form at t = 40:    mean {np.sqrt(abar[39]) * x0:.3f}, std {np.sqrt(1 - abar[39]):.3f}")
print(f"signal left at the final step: sqrt(abar_T) = {np.sqrt(abar[-1]):.3f}")

41.4 The reverse process

To generate, we need to go backwards: \(p(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_t)\). That distribution depends on the whole data distribution, and is unknown. But if we also condition on \(\mathbf{x}_0\), it is a Gaussian that we can write down exactly. By Bayes’ rule (Chapter 22), applied to Gaussians (Chapter 23):

\[ q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_t,\mathbf{x}_0)=\mathcal{N}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t(\mathbf{x}_t,\mathbf{x}_0),\ \tilde\beta_{t}\mathbf{I}\bigr), \tag{41.2}\]

\[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t=\frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\mathbf{x}_0+\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\mathbf{x}_t,\qquad \tilde\beta_t=\frac{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\beta_t. \tag{41.3}\]

TipWhy this form

By Bayes, \(q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_t,\mathbf{x}_0)\propto q(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t-1})\,q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_0)\): a product of two Gaussians in \(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\). The exponent of a product of Gaussians is a sum of quadratics, which is again quadratic. Completing the square gives a precision (inverse variance) that is the sum of the two precisions, \(1/\tilde\beta_t=\alpha_t/\beta_t+1/(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})\), and a mean that is the precision-weighted average of the two means. Simplifying yields Equation 41.3. \(\blacksquare\)

We check the formulas numerically on scalar data: simulate pairs \((\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\mathbf{x}_t)\) from the forward chain, and compare the empirical conditional mean and variance given \(\mathbf{x}_t\) with the formula.

t = 30                                                             # index into arrays (step t+1 in 1-based)
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
x0 = 1.7
x_prev = np.sqrt(abar[t - 1]) * x0 + np.sqrt(1 - abar[t - 1]) * rng.normal(size=2_000_000)   # x_{t-1}

# empirical regression of x_{t-1} on x_t (conditioned on the fixed x0)

41.5 Training: predict the noise

We do not know \(\mathbf{x}_0\) at generation time. We estimate it. From the closed form, \(\mathbf{x}_0=(\mathbf{x}_t-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon})/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\). So knowing the noise \(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\) is equivalent to knowing \(\mathbf{x}_0\). Substituting into Equation 41.3 gives a mean in terms of \(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\):

\[ \tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\Bigl(\mathbf{x}_t-\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\Bigr). \tag{41.4}\]

A network \(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t,t)\) is trained to predict the noise that was added:

\[ \mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0,\ t,\ \boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\Bigl[\bigl\lVert\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta\bigl(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\mathbf{x}_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\ t\bigr)\bigr\rVert^{2}\Bigr]. \tag{41.5}\]

This is a squared-error regression (Chapter 14). It follows from the ELBO of Chapter 40 (the chain of latents \(\mathbf{x}_1,\dots,\mathbf{x}_T\)), where each KL term between two Gaussians reduces to a squared difference of means. Dropping the per-step weights gives Equation 41.5, which works well in practice.

The training algorithm is three lines: sample a data point, a step \(t\), and noise; form \(\mathbf{x}_t\); take a gradient step on the squared error.

41.5.1 The link to the score

The score of a distribution is the gradient of its log-density, \(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\ln p(\mathbf{x})\). For the Gaussian \(q(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_0)\),

\[ \nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t}\ln q(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_0)=-\frac{\mathbf{x}_t-\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\mathbf{x}_0}{1-\bar\alpha_t}=-\frac{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}. \tag{41.6}\]

So the noise prediction is, up to the factor \(-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\), an estimate of the score of the noisy data distribution: the direction in which the density increases most. Denoising is moving up the density.

41.6 A diffusion model on a 2D distribution

We train a diffusion model on the two-moons distribution. The network is an MLP with inputs \((\mathbf{x}_t,t)\) (the step is given a sinusoidal embedding, as in Chapter 31), and output \(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta\in\mathbb{R}^2\).

import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F
from sklearn.datasets import make_moons

def sample_data(n, seed=None):
    X, _ = make_moons(n, noise=0.06, random_state=seed)
    return torch.tensor((X - np.array([0.5, 0.25])) * 2.0, dtype=torch.float32)

class EpsNet(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, hidden=128, temb=16):
        super().__init__()
        self.temb = temb
        self.net = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(2 + temb, hidden), nn.SiLU(), nn.Linear(hidden, hidden), nn.SiLU(),
                                 nn.Linear(hidden, hidden), nn.SiLU(), nn.Linear(hidden, 2))
    def forward(self, x, t):                                   # t: (B,) integer steps
        f = torch.exp(-math.log(1000) * torch.arange(self.temb // 2) / (self.temb // 2))
        a = t[:, None].float() * f[None]
        return self.net(torch.cat([x, a.sin(), a.cos()], dim=1))

abar_t = torch.tensor(abar, dtype=torch.float32)
alphas_t = torch.tensor(alphas, dtype=torch.float32); betas_t = torch.tensor(betas, dtype=torch.float32)

torch.manual_seed(0)
net = EpsNet(); opt = torch.optim.Adam(net.parameters(), lr=2e-3)
losses, t0 = [], time.time()
for step in range(20000):
    x0 = sample_data(256)
    t = torch.randint(0, T, (256,))
    eps = torch.randn_like(x0)
    xt = abar_t[t].sqrt()[:, None] * x0 + (1 - abar_t[t]).sqrt()[:, None] * eps      # closed form
    loss = F.mse_loss(net(xt, t), eps)
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step(); losses.append(loss.item())
print(f"trained in {time.time() - t0:.0f} s; loss {np.mean(losses[:50]):.3f} -> {np.mean(losses[-200:]):.3f}")
trained in 13 s; loss 0.553 -> 0.333

41.6.1 Sampling

DDPM sampling starts from pure noise \(\mathbf{x}_{T}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})\) and applies the learned reverse step \(T\) times:

\[ \mathbf{x}_{t-1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\Bigl(\mathbf{x}_t-\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t,t)\Bigr)+\sigma_{t}\mathbf{z},\qquad\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}). \tag{41.7}\]

DDIM sampling removes the fresh noise and jumps between a sparse subset of steps. First estimate the clean sample, then re-noise it deterministically to the next step:

\[ \hat{\mathbf{x}}_0=\frac{\mathbf{x}_t-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}},\qquad \mathbf{x}_{t'}=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t'}}\,\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t'}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta. \tag{41.8}\]

@torch.no_grad()
def ddpm_sample(n, seed=0, record=False):
    g = torch.Generator().manual_seed(seed)
    x = torch.randn(n, 2, generator=g); traj = [x.clone()]
    for t in reversed(range(T)):
        tt = torch.full((n,), t)
        eps = net(x, tt)
        mean = (x - betas_t[t] / (1 - abar_t[t]).sqrt() * eps) / alphas_t[t].sqrt()
        if t > 0:
            sigma = ((1 - abar_t[t - 1]) / (1 - abar_t[t]) * betas_t[t]).sqrt()
            x = mean + sigma * torch.randn(n, 2, generator=g)
        else:
            x = mean
        if record: traj.append(x.clone())
    return (x, traj) if record else x

@torch.no_grad()
def ddim_sample(n, steps, seed=0):
    g = torch.Generator().manual_seed(seed)
    x = torch.randn(n, 2, generator=g)
    ts = np.linspace(T - 1, 0, steps).round().astype(int)
    for k, t in enumerate(ts):
        eps = net(x, torch.full((n,), int(t)))
        x0_hat = (x - (1 - abar_t[t]).sqrt() * eps) / abar_t[t].sqrt()
        if k + 1 < len(ts):
            a_next = abar_t[ts[k + 1]]
            x = a_next.sqrt() * x0_hat + (1 - a_next).sqrt() * eps
        else:
            x = x0_hat
    return x

def sliced_wasserstein(a, b, n_proj=200, seed=0):
    g = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    dirs = g.normal(size=(n_proj, 2)); dirs /= np.linalg.norm(dirs, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    pa, pb = np.sort(a.numpy() @ dirs.T, axis=0), np.sort(b.numpy() @ dirs.T, axis=0)
    return np.abs(pa - pb).mean()

41.8 Failure modes

Residual signal at the last step. If \(\bar\alpha_T\) is not near zero, \(\mathbf{x}_T\) is not pure noise, but sampling starts from pure noise. The mismatch shows up as washed-out or biased samples. Check \(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_T}\) (it was \(0.05\) here).

Slow sampling. DDPM needs one network evaluation per step, typically hundreds to a thousand. DDIM and higher-order solvers cut this to 10–50 (here, 10 steps were as good as 100 DDPM steps). Very few steps (2 here) degrade quality badly.

Exposure bias. Training sees true noisy inputs. Sampling feeds the model its own outputs. Errors can compound.

Schedule. The noise schedule decides how training effort is spread over noise levels. A linear schedule can spend many steps at nearly pure noise. Cosine schedules and loss re-weighting help.

Prediction target. Predicting \(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\) is unstable at very low and very high noise. Alternatives are \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0\)-prediction and “velocity” prediction.

Mode coverage versus fidelity. A model that matches the density well is not necessarily the one that looks best. Guidance (Chapter 42) trades diversity for fidelity.

Compute. Training and sampling cost grow with image resolution. This is the reason for latent diffusion (Chapter 42).

41.10 System connection

NoteWhat the system gained in this chapter

The system can now generate sharp samples by many small steps. The ingredients are all familiar: Gaussian algebra gives the forward process in closed form, Bayes’ rule gives the reverse step, the loss is a squared-error regression, and a score is a gradient of a log-density. Sampling can be accelerated by deterministic samplers.

The cost is the number of network evaluations, and the size of the images. Chapter 42 makes diffusion cheap by running it in a compressed latent space (the VAE of Chapter 40) and makes it controllable with text through guidance.

Chapter summary

  • Forward: \(\mathbf{x}_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\mathbf{x}_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\).
  • Reverse posterior given \(\mathbf{x}_0\) is Gaussian with mean \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t\) and variance \(\tilde\beta_t\).
  • Training: predict \(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\) with a squared-error loss on random \((\mathbf{x}_0,t,\boldsymbol{\epsilon})\).
  • \(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta\approx-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\nabla\ln q(\mathbf{x}_t)\): the score.
  • DDPM samples with fresh noise at each step. DDIM is deterministic and uses fewer steps.
