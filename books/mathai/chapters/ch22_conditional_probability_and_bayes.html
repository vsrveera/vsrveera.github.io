22  Conditional Probability and Bayes

NoteKey ideas
  • Conditional probability \(P(A\mid B)\) is the probability of \(A\) once we know \(B\) happened.
  • The chain rule factors any joint distribution into a product of conditionals. It is the equation behind language models.
  • Bayes’ theorem turns \(P(B\mid A)\) into \(P(A\mid B)\): from evidence given cause to cause given evidence.
  • Ignoring the base rate (the prior) is the most common error in reasoning about evidence.
  • Naive Bayes assumes features are independent given the class. It is simple, fast, and overconfident when the assumption fails.

22.1 The system problem

Chapter 21 described how uncertain quantities are distributed. A system must also update what it believes when it sees evidence. A spam filter sees words and must decide the class. A language model sees the first words of a sentence and must predict the next.

How should evidence change belief, and how does a joint distribution break into steps we can compute?

22.2 Conditional probability

The probability of \(A\) given \(B\) restricts attention to the cases where \(B\) holds:

\[ P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A,B)}{P(B)},\qquad P(B)>0. \tag{22.1}\]

Rearranged, this is the product rule:

\[ P(A,B)=P(A\mid B)\,P(B)=P(B\mid A)\,P(A). \tag{22.2}\]

We compute a conditional from a joint table. For the two neighbouring digit pixels of Chapter 21 (call them \(A\) and \(B\), on if brightness is above 8):

from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
D = load_digits()
A = (D.data[:, 27] > 8).astype(int); B = (D.data[:, 28] > 8).astype(int)
joint = np.array([[np.mean((A == a) & (B == b)) for b in (0, 1)] for a in (0, 1)])
pA = joint.sum(axis=1)
cond = joint / pA[:, None]                       # P(B = b | A = a), rows sum to 1
print("P(B=1)              =", round(joint[:, 1].sum(), 3))
print("P(B=1 | A=0)        =", round(cond[0, 1], 3))
print("P(B=1 | A=1)        =", round(cond[1, 1], 3), "  <- knowing A changes the belief about B")
P(B=1)              = 0.643
P(B=1 | A=0)        = 0.476
P(B=1 | A=1)        = 0.783   <- knowing A changes the belief about B

22.3 The chain rule

Apply the product rule repeatedly. For any \(n\) variables,

\[ P(x_1,\dots,x_n)=P(x_1)\,P(x_{2}\mid x_1)\,P(x_{3}\mid x_1,x_2)\cdots P(x_{n}\mid x_1,\dots,x_{n-1})=\prod_{t=1}^{n}P(x_{t}\mid x_{<t}). \tag{22.3}\]

TipDerivation by induction

For \(n=2\) it is Equation 22.2. Suppose it holds for \(n-1\) variables. Treat \((x_1,\dots,x_{n-1})\) as one event \(A\) and \(x_n\) as \(B\). Then \(P(A,B)=P(B\mid A)P(A)\), which is \(P(x_{n}\mid x_{<n})\prod_{t<n}P(x_{t}\mid x_{<t})\). \(\blacksquare\)

This is exact. It makes no assumption. It says that the probability of a sequence is the product of the probability of each element given the ones before it. Chapter 33 builds language models on it: the whole model is a machine for \(P(x_{t}\mid x_{<t})\).

We build the smallest word-level language model from the Zen of Python text (Chapter 15): a bigram model, where each word depends only on the previous one. Then we compute the probability of a sentence using the chain rule.

import io, codecs, contextlib
with contextlib.redirect_stdout(io.StringIO()):
    import this
text = codecs.decode(this.s, "rot13").lower()
words = "".join(c if c.isalpha() else " " for c in text).split()
vocab = sorted(set(words)); V = len(vocab)
ix = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(vocab)}
ids = np.array([ix[w] for w in words])
print(f"{len(words)} words, vocabulary {V}")

alpha = 0.1                                       # add-alpha smoothing
C = np.full((V, V), alpha)
np.add.at(C, (ids[:-1], ids[1:]), 1)              # bigram counts
P_next = C / C.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)         # P(next | previous), rows sum to 1
P_first = (np.bincount(ids, minlength=V) + alpha)
P_first = P_first / P_first.sum()                 # P(first word)

def sentence_prob(sentence):
    w = [ix[t] for t in sentence.split()]
    p = P_first[w[0]]
    for a, b in zip(w[:-1], w[1:]):
        p *= P_next[a, b]                         # chain rule, one factor per word
    return p

for s in ["beautiful is better than ugly", "ugly is better than beautiful"]:
    print(f"P('{s}') = {sentence_prob(s):.2e}")
print("rows of P(next|prev) sum to 1:", np.allclose(P_next.sum(axis=1), 1))
print("all two-word sequences sum to 1:",
      np.isclose((P_first[:, None] * P_next).sum(), 1.0))
147 words, vocabulary 87
P('beautiful is better than ugly') = 9.72e-06
P('ugly is better than beautiful') = 8.03e-08
rows of P(next|prev) sum to 1: True
all two-word sequences sum to 1: True

The model assigns a much higher probability to the first sentence, which appears in the text, than to the reordered version. Note the last line: the probabilities of all two-word sequences sum to 1. The chain rule gives a valid distribution.

22.4 Bayes’ theorem

Equate the two forms of the product rule and divide:

\[ P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(B\mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B)},\qquad P(B)=\sum_aP(B\mid a)\,P(a). \tag{22.4}\]

Term Name Meaning
\(P(A)\) prior belief before the evidence
\(P(B\mid A)\) likelihood how likely the evidence is if \(A\) is true
\(P(A\mid B)\) posterior belief after the evidence
\(P(B)\) evidence (marginal) how likely the evidence is overall

In a diagnosis problem, the cause is the disease and the evidence is the test. We usually know \(P(\text{test}\mid\text{disease})\) from lab studies. We want \(P(\text{disease}\mid\text{test})\).

22.4.1 The base-rate trap

A disease affects 1% of people. A test detects it in 90% of sick people (sensitivity). It is negative in 95% of healthy people (specificity). A person tests positive. How likely are they to be sick?

Most people guess about 90%. The right answer is far lower.

\[ P(\text{sick}\mid+)=\frac{0.90\cdot0.01}{0.90\cdot0.01+0.05\cdot0.99}\approx0.154. \]

The reason is simple in counts. In 2,000 people, 20 are sick and 18 test positive. Among the 1,980 healthy, 5% (99) also test positive. Of 117 positives, only 18 are sick.

n, prev, sens, spec = 2000, 0.01, 0.90, 0.95
sick = int(n * prev); healthy = n - sick
tp = int(round(sick * sens)); fn = sick - tp
fp = int(round(healthy * (1 - spec))); tn = healthy - fp
print(f"sick {sick}: positive {tp}, negative {fn};  healthy {healthy}: positive {fp}, negative {tn}")
print(f"P(sick | positive) = {tp / (tp + fp):.3f}")

codes = np.array([0] * tn + [1] * fn + [2] * fp + [3] * tp)       # 0 healthy-neg ... 3 sick-pos
grid = codes.reshape(40, 50)
from matplotlib.colors import ListedColormap
cmap = ListedColormap(["#E5E7EB", "#9CA3AF", PALETTE["model"], PALETTE["loss"]])
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 3.6))
ax.imshow(grid, cmap=cmap, aspect="auto"); ax.set_xticks([]); ax.set_yticks([]); ax.grid(False)
labels = [("healthy, negative", "#E5E7EB"), ("sick, negative (missed)", "#9CA3AF"),
          ("healthy, positive (false alarm)", PALETTE["model"]), ("sick, positive", PALETTE["loss"])]
for i, (lab, c) in enumerate(labels):
    ax.plot([], [], "s", color=c, label=lab)
ax.legend(loc="upper center", bbox_to_anchor=(0.5, -0.02), ncol=2, fontsize=8, frameon=False)
plt.show()
sick 20: positive 18, negative 2;  healthy 1980: positive 99, negative 1881
P(sick | positive) = 0.154

We confirm it by simulation.

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
N = 2_000_000
is_sick = rng.random(N) < prev
positive = np.where(is_sick, rng.random(N) < sens, rng.random(N) < 1 - spec)
print(f"simulated P(sick | positive) = {is_sick[positive].mean():.3f}  (Bayes: {tp / (tp + fp):.3f})")
simulated P(sick | positive) = 0.153  (Bayes: 0.154)

22.5 Updating beliefs: the Beta–Binomial model

Bayes’ theorem can be applied again and again as data arrives. The posterior after one observation is the prior for the next.

Take a coin with unknown heads probability \(\theta\). We flip it \(n\) times and see \(k\) heads. The likelihood is \(\theta^k(1-\theta)^{n-k}\). Use a Beta prior, \(p(\theta)\propto\theta^{a-1}(1-\theta)^{b-1}\). By Bayes,

\[ p(\theta\mid k)\;\propto\;\underbrace{\theta^{k}(1-\theta)^{n-k}}_{\text{likelihood}}\;\underbrace{\theta^{a-1}(1-\theta)^{b-1}}_{\text{prior}}=\theta^{a+k-1}(1-\theta)^{b+n-k-1}. \tag{22.5}\]

The posterior is again a Beta, with parameters \((a+k,\ b+n-k)\). The prior is conjugate: the update only adds the counts. The posterior mean is \(\frac{a+k}{a+b+n}\). Think of \(a,b\) as “pseudo-counts” of heads and tails.

from scipy.stats import beta
rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
flips = (rng.random(100) < 0.7).astype(int)
ths = np.linspace(0, 1, 400)
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 3.4), sharey=True)
for ax, (a0, b0), title in zip(axes, [(1, 1), (20, 20)], ["flat prior Beta(1, 1)", "strong prior Beta(20, 20)"]):
    for n_obs, c in zip([0, 1, 5, 20, 100], ["#9CA3AF", PALETTE["data"], PALETTE["prob"], PALETTE["model"], PALETTE["loss"]]):
        k = flips[:n_obs].sum()
        ax.plot(ths, beta.pdf(ths, a0 + k, b0 + n_obs - k), color=c, lw=2, label=f"n = {n_obs}")
    ax.axvline(0.7, ls="--", color="black", lw=0.8); ax.set_title(title); ax.set_xlabel("θ")
axes[0].legend(fontsize=8); axes[0].set_ylabel("posterior density")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
for (a0, b0) in [(1, 1), (20, 20)]:
    k = flips[:10].sum()
    print(f"prior Beta({a0},{b0}), after 10 flips ({k} heads): posterior mean {(a0 + k) / (a0 + b0 + 10):.3f}")
prior Beta(1,1), after 10 flips (8 heads): posterior mean 0.750
prior Beta(20,20), after 10 flips (8 heads): posterior mean 0.560

With little data the prior dominates. With much data the likelihood dominates and the prior is forgotten. This is how Bayesian reasoning behaves, and it is the idea behind regularisation (Chapter 25).

22.6 Naive Bayes: a spam filter

Chapter 1 opened with a spam filter. We can now build one. Let \(c\) be the class (spam or ham) and \(\mathbf{w}=(w_1,\dots,w_m)\) the words of a message. By Bayes,

\[ P(c\mid\mathbf{w})\;\propto\;P(c)\,P(\mathbf{w}\mid c). \]

The likelihood \(P(\mathbf{w}\mid c)\) of a whole message is hard to estimate. Naive Bayes assumes the words are independent given the class:

\[ P(\mathbf{w}\mid c)\approx\prod_{j=1}^{m}P(w_{j}\mid c). \tag{22.6}\]

Each factor is a word frequency in that class, with add-one smoothing:

\[ P(w\mid c)=\frac{\text{count}(w,c)+1}{\sum_{w'}\text{count}(w',c)+|V|}. \tag{22.7}\]

We compute in log space (sums, not products), to avoid underflow.

spam = ["win a free prize now", "free money click here now", "claim your free gift card today",
        "limited offer win cash prize", "urgent your account is suspended click here",
        "cheap pills online buy now", "you have won a free vacation claim now",
        "exclusive deal buy cheap watches today", "congratulations you won cash click now",
        "earn money fast work from home", "free entry win a prize text now",
        "final notice claim your cash prize"]
ham = ["are we still meeting for lunch today", "please review the report before the meeting",
       "can you send me the notes from class", "dinner at my place on friday",
       "the project deadline is moved to next week", "thanks for your help with the presentation",
       "i will call you after the meeting", "let us go for a walk this evening",
       "did you see the game last night", "happy birthday hope you have a great day",
       "the report is attached please review it", "see you at the office tomorrow morning"]

vocab_nb = sorted({w for m in spam + ham for w in m.split()})
ix_nb = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(vocab_nb)}
def counts(msgs):
    c = np.zeros(len(vocab_nb))
    for m in msgs:
        for w in m.split():
            c[ix_nb[w]] += 1
    return c

cs, ch = counts(spam), counts(ham)
logp_w = {"spam": np.log((cs + 1) / (cs.sum() + len(vocab_nb))),
          "ham":  np.log((ch + 1) / (ch.sum() + len(vocab_nb)))}
log_prior = {"spam": np.log(len(spam) / (len(spam) + len(ham))),
             "ham":  np.log(len(ham) / (len(spam) + len(ham)))}

def p_spam(message):
    known = [ix_nb[w] for w in message.split() if w in ix_nb]       # ignore unseen words
    s = log_prior["spam"] + sum(logp_w["spam"][i] for i in known)
    h = log_prior["ham"] + sum(logp_w["ham"][i] for i in known)
    return 1 / (1 + np.exp(h - s))                                   # normalise two classes

tests = [("free cash prize waiting for you", "spam"), ("claim your free vacation now", "spam"),
         ("please send the report before lunch", "ham"), ("are you free for dinner tomorrow", "ham"),
         ("win money now", "spam"), ("meeting notes attached for review", "ham")]
print(f"{'message':40s} {'P(spam)':>8s}  truth")
for m, truth in tests:
    print(f"{m:40s} {p_spam(m):8.3f}  {truth}")
message                                   P(spam)  truth
free cash prize waiting for you             0.958  spam
claim your free vacation now                0.999  spam
please send the report before lunch         0.002  ham
are you free for dinner tomorrow            0.135  ham
win money now                               0.991  spam
meeting notes attached for review           0.008  ham

Which words carry the decision? The log-odds of a word is \(\ln\frac{P(w\mid\text{spam})}{P(w\mid\text{ham})}\).

lo = logp_w["spam"] - logp_w["ham"]
order = np.argsort(lo)
pick = np.r_[order[:10], order[-10:]]
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(5.6, 4.2))
ax.barh([vocab_nb[i] for i in pick], lo[pick],
        color=[PALETTE["loss"] if v > 0 else PALETTE["data"] for v in lo[pick]])
ax.axvline(0, color="black", lw=0.8); ax.set_xlabel("log-odds  ln P(w|spam) − ln P(w|ham)")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

22.6.1 When the independence assumption fails

Words are not independent. In “free cash prize”, the three words are all signs of the same thing. Naive Bayes counts each as new evidence. The posterior becomes over-confident.

We can see it by repeating a message. Repeating it adds no information, but multiplies the likelihood ratios.

msg = "are you free for dinner tomorrow"           # truly ham, contains one spammy word
for reps in [1, 2, 4, 8]:
    print(f"message repeated {reps}x: P(spam) = {p_spam(' '.join([msg] * reps)):.4f}")
msg2 = "free prize for the report"                 # mixed evidence
print()
for reps in [1, 2, 4, 8]:
    print(f"mixed message repeated {reps}x: P(spam) = {p_spam(' '.join([msg2] * reps)):.4f}")
message repeated 1x: P(spam) = 0.1347
message repeated 2x: P(spam) = 0.0237
message repeated 4x: P(spam) = 0.0006
message repeated 8x: P(spam) = 0.0000

mixed message repeated 1x: P(spam) = 0.2762
mixed message repeated 2x: P(spam) = 0.1271
mixed message repeated 4x: P(spam) = 0.0208
mixed message repeated 8x: P(spam) = 0.0004

Evidence that is only repeated pushes the probability towards 0 or 1. Naive Bayes is often a good classifier (the ranking is right) and a poor probability estimator (the numbers are too extreme). Chapter 24 shows how to calibrate probabilities.

22.7 Production

scikit-learn provides the same classifier. We check that it matches ours.

from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import CountVectorizer
from sklearn.naive_bayes import MultinomialNB

cv = CountVectorizer(vocabulary=vocab_nb, token_pattern=r"\b\w+\b")
Xtr = cv.transform(spam + ham); ytr = np.array([1] * len(spam) + [0] * len(ham))
nb = MultinomialNB(alpha=1.0).fit(Xtr, ytr)
Xq = cv.transform([m for m, _ in tests])
ours = np.array([p_spam(m) for m, _ in tests])
print("sklearn P(spam):", np.round(nb.predict_proba(Xq)[:, 1], 3))
print("ours           :", np.round(ours, 3))
print("match:", np.allclose(nb.predict_proba(Xq)[:, 1], ours))
sklearn P(spam): [0.958 0.999 0.002 0.135 0.991 0.008]

22.8 Failure modes

Base-rate neglect. A good test applied to a rare condition gives mostly false positives. Always ask: what is the prior?

Zero counts. A word never seen in spam gives \(P(w\mid\text{spam})=0\) and kills the whole product. Smoothing (add-one or add-\(\alpha\)) fixes this.

Violated independence. Strongly dependent features give over-confident posteriors (shown above).

Prior sensitivity. With little data, the posterior depends on the prior (Figure 40.4). State the prior and test how much the conclusion changes.

Underflow. A product of many small probabilities underflows to 0. Work with log-probabilities.

Conditioning on rare events. If \(P(B)\) is tiny, estimates of \(P(A\mid B)\) come from few samples and are unreliable.

22.10 System connection

NoteWhat the system gained in this chapter

The system can now condition and update: it can ask what a piece of evidence implies, break a joint distribution into steps by the chain rule, and combine a prior with a likelihood. It has the equation \(P(x_{1:n})=\prod_tP(x_{t}\mid x_{<t})\) that language models are built on.

Next, Chapter 23 lists the few distributions that appear almost everywhere: Bernoulli, categorical, Gaussian, and their relatives.

Chapter summary

  • \(P(A\mid B)=P(A,B)/P(B)\). The product rule: \(P(A,B)=P(A\mid B)P(B)\).
  • Chain rule: \(P(x_{1:n})=\prod_tP(x_{t}\mid x_{<t})\). It is exact.
  • Bayes: posterior \(\propto\) likelihood \(\times\) prior.
  • With a rare condition, a positive test is mostly a false alarm.
  • The Beta prior is conjugate to the Binomial: the update adds counts.
  • Naive Bayes multiplies per-word likelihoods. Smoothing avoids zeros. Dependent features make it over-confident.
