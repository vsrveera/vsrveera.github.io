4  Geometry of Representations

NoteKey ideas
  • A norm gives the length of a vector. A distance is the norm of a difference.
  • The dot product measures how much two vectors point the same way: \(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=\lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert\cos\theta\).
  • Cosine similarity keeps only the angle. It ignores length.
  • Projection splits a vector into a part along a direction and a part orthogonal to it.
  • In high dimension, random vectors are almost orthogonal. This is useful and also dangerous.

4.1 The system problem

Chapter 3 gave every object a place in a space. A place alone does not help a model. The model must answer questions about places:

  • How big is this vector?
  • How close are these two vectors?
  • Do these two vectors point the same way?

Given two vectors, how do we say that one is longer, or that two are similar?

This is the geometry of representations. It powers System B (semantic search) directly: search is the question “which stored vectors are most similar to the query vector?”

4.2 Length: norms

A norm assigns a non-negative length \(\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert\) to each vector. It must satisfy three rules:

  1. \(\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert = 0\) only for \(\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\).
  2. \(\lVert c\,\mathbf{x}\rVert = |c|\,\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert\).
  3. \(\lVert\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}\rVert \le \lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert + \lVert\mathbf{y}\rVert\) (triangle inequality).

Three norms are common. All are special cases of one family, \(\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_p = (\sum_i |x_i|^p)^{1/p}\).

Norm Formula Meaning Where used
\(\ell_2\) (Euclidean) \(\sqrt{\sum x_i^2}\) straight-line length default; MSE; normalization
\(\ell_1\) (Manhattan) \(\sum \lvert x_{i}\rvert\) sum of absolute steps L1 regularization; sparsity
\(\ell_\infty\) (max) \(\max_i \lvert x_{i}\rvert\) largest component worst-case error 
x = np.array([3.0, -4.0, 0.0, 12.0])

# plain Python
l2_py = sum(v * v for v in x) ** 0.5
l1_py = sum(abs(v) for v in x)

# NumPy
print("l2  :", l2_py, np.linalg.norm(x))              # 13.0
print("l1  :", l1_py, np.linalg.norm(x, 1))           # 19.0
print("linf:", max(abs(x)), np.linalg.norm(x, np.inf))
l2  : 13.0 13.0
l1  : 19.0 19.0
linf: 12.0 12.0

4.3 Distance

The distance between \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) is the norm of their difference:

\[ d(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}) = \lVert\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}\rVert. \tag{4.1}\]

With the \(\ell_2\) norm this is the straight-line distance. Retrieval, clustering, and nearest-neighbour methods all start here. Chapter 13 develops more distances.

4.4 The dot product

The dot product of \(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^d\) is

\[ \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{a}^{\intercal}\mathbf{b} = \sum_{i=1}^{d} a_i b_i. \tag{4.2}\]

It is one multiplication per component, then one sum. It is the most-used operation in AI. Every neuron, every attention score, and every similarity search computes one.

The sum has a geometric meaning:

\[ \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = \lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert\cos\theta \tag{4.3}\]

where \(\theta\) is the angle between the vectors. We prove this now.

TipDerivation: the dot product and the angle

Place \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) at the origin. They form two sides of a triangle. The third side is \(\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}\). The law of cosines says

\[ \lVert\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}\rVert^2 = \lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert^2 + \lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert^2 - 2\lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert\cos\theta. \]

Now expand the left side with components:

\[ \lVert\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}\rVert^2 = \sum_i (a_i-b_i)^2 = \lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert^2 + \lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert^2 - 2\sum_i a_ib_i. \]

The first two terms are equal on both sides. So \(\sum_i a_ib_i = \lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert\cos\theta\). \(\blacksquare\)

Three cases give the dot product its meaning:

Angle \(\cos\theta\) \(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}\) Reading
\(0^\circ\) \(1\) largest positive same direction
\(90^\circ\) \(0\) \(0\) orthogonal: unrelated
\(180^\circ\) \(-1\) largest negative opposite direction 
a = np.array([3.0, 1.0]); b = np.array([1.0, 2.5])
theta = np.arccos(a @ b / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b)))
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(4.8, 3.6))
setup_2d(ax, xlim=(-0.5, 4), ylim=(-0.5, 3.2))

draw_arrow(ax, b, color=PALETTE["prob"], label="b")

           label="a − b", label_offset=(-0.2, 0.15))
arc = np.linspace(np.arctan2(a[1], a[0]), np.arctan2(b[1], b[0]), 30)
ax.plot(0.7 * np.cos(arc), 0.7 * np.sin(arc), color=PALETTE["neutral"])
ax.text(0.85, 0.55, f"θ = {np.degrees(theta):.0f}°", color=PALETTE["neutral"])
plt.show()
lengths = np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b)
print("a·b =", a @ b, "   |a||b|cosθ =", lengths * np.cos(theta))

4.5 Cosine similarity

Solving Equation 4.3 for \(\cos\theta\) gives the cosine similarity:

\[ \cos(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}) = \frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{\lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert} \tag{4.4}\]

It always lies in \([-1, 1]\). It is \(1\) for the same direction, \(0\) for orthogonal vectors, and \(-1\) for opposite ones. Doubling a vector does not change it. Cosine similarity compares direction and ignores length.

That the value lies in \([-1,1]\) is not obvious from Equation 4.4. It follows from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality: \(|\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}| \le \lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert\).

TipDerivation: Cauchy–Schwarz

For any real number \(t\), the squared length of \(\mathbf{a}-t\mathbf{b}\) is non-negative:

\[ 0 \le \lVert\mathbf{a}-t\mathbf{b}\rVert^2 = \lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert^2 - 2t\,\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} + t^{2}\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert^2. \]

The right side is a quadratic in \(t\). It is never negative. So its discriminant is not positive:

\[ 4(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b})^2 - 4\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert^{2}\lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert^2 \le 0 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; |\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}| \le \lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert. \qquad\blacksquare \]

4.6 Projection and orthogonality

How much of \(\mathbf{a}\) lies along the direction of \(\mathbf{b}\)? The answer is the projection:

\[ \text{proj}_{\mathbf{b}}\mathbf{a} = \frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{b}}\,\mathbf{b}. \tag{4.5}\]

The remainder \(\mathbf{a}-\text{proj}_{\mathbf{b}}\mathbf{a}\) is orthogonal to \(\mathbf{b}\). So every vector splits into two parts: one along \(\mathbf{b}\) and one orthogonal to it.

a = np.array([3.0, 2.0]); b = np.array([4.0, 0.5])
p = (a @ b) / (b @ b) * b
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(5, 3.6))

draw_arrow(ax, a, color=PALETTE["data"], label="a")

4.6.1 Coordinates in an orthonormal basis

We can now keep a promise from Chapter 3. A basis is orthonormal if its vectors have length 1 and are pairwise orthogonal. In such a basis, the coordinates need no equation solving:

\[ \mathbf{v}=\sum_i c_i\,\mathbf{b}_i \quad\Longrightarrow\quad c_i = \mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{b}_i. \tag{4.6}\]

Proof. Take the dot product of \(\mathbf{v}\) with \(\mathbf{b}_j\). Every term with \(i\ne j\) is \(0\). The term \(i=j\) gives \(c_j\,\mathbf{b}_{j}\cdot\mathbf{b}_j = c_j\). \(\blacksquare\)

Gram–Schmidt builds an orthonormal basis from any independent list: subtract the projections onto the vectors already chosen, then normalize.

def gram_schmidt(vectors):
    basis = []
    for v in vectors:
        w = v.astype(float).copy()
        for b in basis:
            w -= (w @ b) * b                  # remove the component along b
        basis.append(w / np.linalg.norm(w))   # normalize
    return np.array(basis)                    # (k, d)

V = np.array([[1.0, 1.0, 0.0], [1.0, 0.0, 1.0], [0.0, 1.0, 1.0]])
Q = gram_schmidt(V)
print("Q Q^T =\n", np.round(Q @ Q.T, 10))     # identity ⇒ orthonormal

v = np.array([2.0, -1.0, 3.0])
c = Q @ v                                     # coordinates: c_i = v · b_i
print("reconstruction error:", np.linalg.norm(Q.T @ c - v))
Q Q^T =
 [[ 1.  0.  0.]
 [ 0.  1. -0.]
 [ 0. -0.  1.]]
reconstruction error: 9.155133597044475e-16

4.7 High dimensions: almost everything is orthogonal

Take two random directions in 2D. They can easily be close in angle. Take two in 1000 dimensions. Their angle is almost always very close to \(90^\circ\).

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 3.4))
colors = [PALETTE[k] for k in ["loss", "model", "grad", "data"]]
for d, col in zip([2, 10, 100, 1000], colors):
    U = rng.normal(size=(3000, d)); W = rng.normal(size=(3000, d))
    norms = np.linalg.norm(U, axis=1) * np.linalg.norm(W, axis=1)
    cos = np.sum(U * W, axis=1) / norms
    ax.hist(np.degrees(np.arccos(cos)), bins=60, range=(0, 180), alpha=0.55,
            color=col, label=f"d = {d}", density=True)
ax.set_xlabel("angle between two random vectors (degrees)")
ax.set_ylabel("density")
ax.legend()
plt.show()

This has two consequences.

  • Good news. A \(d\)-dimensional space has room for many nearly-orthogonal directions. A model can store many independent features in one space.
  • Bad news. A random vector gives a cosine near \(0\) with everything. A cosine of \(0.1\) is not weak evidence in 1000 dimensions. It is a very large departure from the random baseline of about \(1/\sqrt{d}\).

4.8 Computation: similarity search

System B is a similarity search: given a query, rank the stored vectors by similarity. We build it with a bag-of-words representation. Each document is a vector of word counts.

docs = ["cat sat on mat",
        "dog sat on mat",
        "cat and dog are pet",
        "stock market fell today",
        "pet food sale pet food store sells pet food for dog and cat food "
        "and pet toys and pet beds and food bowls and pet food"]
vocab = sorted({w for d in docs for w in d.split()})

def to_vec(text):
    v = np.zeros(len(vocab))                # (V,)
    for w in text.split():
        if w in vocab:
            v[vocab.index(w)] += 1
    return v

D = np.array([to_vec(d) for d in docs])     # (5, V)
q = to_vec("cat pet")                       # (V,)

dot = D @ q                                                      # (5,)
cos = dot / (np.linalg.norm(D, axis=1) * np.linalg.norm(q))     # (5,)
print("dot product ranking:", np.argsort(-dot))
print("cosine ranking     :", np.argsort(-cos))
print("cosine scores      :", np.round(cos, 3))
dot product ranking: [4 2 0 1 3]
cosine ranking     : [2 4 0 1 3]
cosine scores      : [0.354 0.    0.632 0.    0.481]

The two rankings differ. The long document (number 4) repeats “pet” many times. The raw dot product rewards length. Document 2 is the shorter and better match, and cosine puts it first.

Document Dot product Cosine Reading
2: “cat and dog are pet” 2 0.63 short, on topic
4: long pet-food text 7 0.48 long, partly on topic

Which is correct depends on the application. In text retrieval, we usually want direction (topic), not length. For some recommenders, length carries meaning, such as popularity, and the raw dot product is intended.

4.8.1 Search over images

The same code works for any vector. We apply it to the digit vectors of Chapter 3: for each image, find its nearest neighbour among all other images, and check whether the labels agree.

from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
digits = load_digits()
X, y = digits.data, digits.target                     # (1797, 64), (1797,)

Xn = X / np.linalg.norm(X, axis=1, keepdims=True)     # unit vectors
S = Xn @ Xn.T                                         # (1797, 1797) all cosines
np.fill_diagonal(S, -np.inf)                          # exclude self-match

acc_cos = np.mean(y[S.argmax(axis=1)] == y)           # leave-one-out 1-NN
sq = (X ** 2).sum(axis=1)
D2 = sq[:, None] + sq[None, :] - 2 * X @ X.T          # squared distances
np.fill_diagonal(D2, np.inf)
acc_euc = np.mean(y[D2.argmin(axis=1)] == y)
print(f"1-NN accuracy  cosine: {acc_cos:.3f}   euclidean: {acc_euc:.3f}")

fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 6, figsize=(9, 3.2))
for row, qi in enumerate([7, 100]):
    top = np.argsort(-S[qi])[:5]
    for col, idx in enumerate([qi, *top]):
        ax = axes[row, col]
        ax.imshow(digits.images[idx], cmap="gray_r")
        ax.set_xticks([]); ax.set_yticks([]); ax.grid(False)
        ax.set_title("query" if col == 0 else f"{S[qi, idx]:.2f}", fontsize=9)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
1-NN accuracy  cosine: 0.989   euclidean: 0.988

Simple geometry on raw pixels already gives about 99% accuracy on this small dataset. This is a baseline that learned representations must beat on harder data.

Note the trick in D2: the squared distance expands to \(\lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert^2+\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert^2-2\,\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}\). All pairwise distances come from one matrix product. Production search systems use this fact.

means = np.array([X[y == c].mean(axis=0) for c in range(10)])    # (10, 64)
Mn = means / np.linalg.norm(means, axis=1, keepdims=True)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(5, 4.3))
im = ax.imshow(Mn @ Mn.T, cmap="viridis", vmin=0.3, vmax=1)
ax.set_xticks(range(10)); ax.set_yticks(range(10)); ax.grid(False)
ax.set_xlabel("class"); ax.set_ylabel("class"); plt.colorbar(im, ax=ax)
plt.show()

4.9 Embedding geometry in practice

Learned embeddings have a geometry that the designer must understand.

  • Normalized embeddings. Many models output unit-length vectors. Then \(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=\cos\theta\), and dot product and cosine are the same. Inner-product search becomes cosine search.
  • Direction carries meaning. In Chapter 3’s toy embedding, the direction “man → woman” was constant. Projection onto such a direction extracts one attribute.
  • Anisotropy. Trained language-model embeddings often occupy a narrow cone, not the whole sphere. Then all cosines are high, and small differences matter. Centering the vectors can restore contrast.

4.10 From first principles to production

PyTorch provides these operations directly. The results match our code.

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

A = torch.tensor(D, dtype=torch.float32)             # (5, V)
qt = torch.tensor(q, dtype=torch.float32)            # (V,)
cos_t = F.cosine_similarity(A, qt.unsqueeze(0), dim=1)   # (5,)
print("matches NumPy:", np.allclose(cos_t.numpy(), cos, atol=1e-6))

# normalize once, then search is one matrix product
An = F.normalize(A, dim=1)
scores = An @ F.normalize(qt, dim=0)
print("ranking:", torch.argsort(scores, descending=True).tolist())
matches NumPy: True
ranking: [2, 4, 0, 1, 3]

4.11 Failure modes

Cosine of a zero vector. The formula divides by \(\lVert\mathbf{0}\rVert=0\). A document with no known words produces nan. Add a small \(\epsilon\) or test for zero length.

z = np.zeros(3); w = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 3.0])
with np.errstate(invalid="ignore"):
    print("cosine(0, w) =", (z @ w) / (np.linalg.norm(z) * np.linalg.norm(w)))
cosine(0, w) = nan

Length that carries meaning. Cosine discards the norm. If the norm encodes confidence or frequency, throwing it away loses information.

Hubness. In high dimension, a few vectors become the nearest neighbour of many queries. Search quality drops. Centering and normalization reduce the effect.

Scale mismatch. If one feature has a large range, it dominates the dot product and the distance. Standardize features first (Chapter 3).

4.13 System connection

NoteWhat the system gained in this chapter

The system can now measure: length, distance, angle, and similarity. System B has its first complete pipeline: text → vector → similarity → ranked results.

Vectors are still passive objects. Chapter 5 adds matrices, which move vectors from one space to another.

Chapter summary

  • A norm gives length. Distance is the norm of a difference. \(\ell_1,\ell_2,\ell_\infty\) differ in their unit balls.
  • \(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=\sum a_ib_i=\lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert\cos\theta\).
  • Cosine similarity compares direction only. It lies in \([-1,1]\) by Cauchy–Schwarz.
  • Projection splits a vector into an along-part and an orthogonal part.
  • In an orthonormal basis, the coordinates are dot products.
  • In high dimension, random vectors are nearly orthogonal.
  • All pairwise distances come from one matrix product.
