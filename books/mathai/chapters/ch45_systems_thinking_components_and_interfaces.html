45  Systems Thinking: Components, Interfaces and Contracts

NoteKey ideas
  • A system is a set of components joined by interfaces, built to meet a requirement. Its behaviour comes from the parts and from the way they are joined.
  • A model is one component. The data pipeline, the pre-processing, the post-processing and the human who reads the output are components too.
  • An interface contract states what a component expects and what it promises. Most field failures are broken contracts, not bad models.
  • End-to-end success is not the product of stage success rates when stage errors are correlated. Measure stages separately and together.

45.1 The system problem

Parts I to VIII studied one mathematical component at a time: a vector space, a loss, an optimiser, an attention layer. Chapter 44 joined many of them into one system. A customer does not buy an attention layer. A customer buys a result: a correct reading of an invoice, a safe lane change, a diagnosis that is right often enough to trust.

If every component is good, is the system good? And if the system fails, which component do we repair?

The answer is “not necessarily” to the first question. The second question needs a method. Part IX gives that method. It has two sources.

  1. Systems thinking. We describe the whole as parts, interfaces, feedback and requirements. This chapter and the next one do that.
  2. Performance characterisation. We measure how a component or a system behaves as its input and its parameters change, and we give the answer as a distribution with a stated confidence, not as one number. Chapters 47 to 50 do that. The approach follows the work of Robert M. Haralick and Visvanathan Ramesh in computer vision (see the references at the end of Chapter 47).

45.2 What a system is

A system is a set of components \(\{C_1,\dots,C_m\}\), a set of interfaces that join them, and a purpose. We write a chain of components as a composition (Chapter 8):

\[ y = (C_m\circ C_{m-1}\circ\cdots\circ C_1)(x). \tag{45.1}\]

This is the same idea as a neural network (Chapter 11). The difference is that the components are not all differentiable layers. Some are cameras, thresholds, look-up tables, rules, queues and people. We cannot back-propagate through a human review step, so we must characterise it by measurement.

Three questions describe any component.

Question Answer is called
What input does it accept? Pre-condition (its input domain)
What output does it produce? Post-condition (its output range)
How well, and under which conditions? Performance characterisation

The third row is the subject of the next chapters. The first two rows are the contract.

45.3 Requirements first

A requirement is a statement that a test can decide. “The detector must be accurate” is not a requirement. “On daytime highway images with rain, at most 1 in 10,000 vehicles is missed and at most 1 in 100 frames contains a false alarm, with 95% confidence” is one.

A good requirement has four parts:

  1. an operating condition (which inputs),
  2. a measure (which quantity),
  3. a level (which number), and
  4. a confidence (how sure we must be that we met it).

Part 4 is the one most often left out. It decides how much test data we need (Chapter 49).

45.4 Series reliability and the correlation trap

Suppose stage \(k\) gives a correct result with probability \(p_k\). If a correct final answer needs every stage to be correct, and stage failures are independent, then

\[ P(\text{system correct})=\prod_{k=1}^{m}p_k. \tag{45.2}\]

Three stages with \(p=(0.95,\,0.90,\,0.92)\) give \(0.95\times0.90\times0.92=0.787\). The system is worse than its worst stage. This is the first system effect: more components, less reliability, unless the interfaces repair errors.

Equation 45.2 assumes independence. Real stages are not independent. A blurred, dark or unusual input is hard for all stages. We model this with one shared latent “difficulty” per input. The inputs that are hard (30% of them) fail 2.5 times as often as average at every stage. The marginal success rate of each stage stays the same.

rng = np.random.default_rng(45)
N = 200_000
p = np.array([0.95, 0.90, 0.92])
print("product of stage accuracies      ", p.prod().round(4))

independent = (rng.random((N, 3)) < p).all(1).mean()
print("simulated, independent failures  ", independent.round(4))

hard = rng.random(N) < 0.30          # 30% of inputs are hard for every stage
k_hard = 2.5                          # hard inputs fail 2.5x as often as average
k_easy = (1 - 0.30 * k_hard) / 0.70   # keeps the average failure rate unchanged
q = 1 - p
p_stage = np.where(hard[:, None], 1 - q * k_hard, 1 - q * k_easy)
ok = rng.random((N, 3)) < p_stage

print("stage success rates              ", ok.mean(0).round(4))
print("simulated, shared difficulty     ", ok.all(1).mean().round(4))
product of stage accuracies       0.7866
simulated, independent failures   0.7868
stage success rates               [0.9502 0.8994 0.9186]
simulated, shared difficulty      0.8004

Each stage keeps its measured success rate. The system is more reliable than the product predicts: \(0.800\) against \(0.787\). Failures cluster on the same inputs, so fewer inputs fail overall.

This is not a reason to relax. The same effect hides the worst case. The failures are concentrated on the hard 30% of the inputs, where the system is much less reliable than its average.

succ = ok.all(1)
vals = [p.prod(), independent, succ[~hard].mean(), succ[hard].mean()]
names = ["product\nformula", "independent\nsimulation", "easy inputs\n(shared difficulty)", "hard inputs\n(shared difficulty)"]
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.4, 3.4))
ax.bar(names, vals, color=[PALETTE["neutral"], PALETTE["neutral"], PALETTE["grad"], PALETTE["loss"]])
for i, v in enumerate(vals):
    ax.text(i, v + 0.01, f"{v:.3f}", ha="center")
ax.set_ylim(0, 1.05); ax.set_ylabel("P(system correct)")
plt.show()
print("easy:", succ[~hard].mean().round(3), " hard:", succ[hard].mean().round(3))
easy: 0.92  hard: 0.523

The lesson: the average hides the structure. Characterise performance by condition (Chapter 47), not only by its mean.

45.5 Locating a failure

An end-to-end score tells us that the system fails. It does not tell us where. To find the stage, we need a test for each stage with known good input. The data above shows how the information is distributed.

failed = ~ok.all(1)
share = (~ok)[failed].mean(0)
for k, s in enumerate(share, 1):
    print(f"stage {k}: fails in {s:.1%} of the system failures")
print("stage shares sum to", share.sum().round(3), "(a failure can involve more than one stage)")
stage 1: fails in 25.0% of the system failures
stage 2: fails in 50.4% of the system failures
stage 3: fails in 40.8% of the system failures
stage shares sum to 1.161 (a failure can involve more than one stage)

Stage 2 is involved in about half of all system failures, although its accuracy of \(0.90\) is only a little worse than the others. The share depends on the accuracy and on how often the stage runs with a bad input from an earlier stage. A stage-by-stage report with a fixed, trusted input gives the stage’s own accuracy. A report from the running system gives the conditional accuracy. Both are needed.

45.6 Interface contracts

An interface passes data between components. The data has a shape, a type, a unit, a range and a distribution. If the producer and the consumer disagree on any of these, no exception is raised in most numerical code. The result is a plausible but wrong number.

We make the contract executable. The check costs little and it turns a silent error into a loud one.

import numpy as np

class Contract:
    """Pre/post-condition for an array that crosses an interface."""
    def __init__(self, name, shape, lo, hi, dtype=np.floating):
        self.name, self.shape, self.lo, self.hi, self.dtype = name, shape, lo, hi, dtype
    def check(self, a):
        a = np.asarray(a)
        if a.ndim != len(self.shape) or any(s is not None and s != d for s, d in zip(self.shape, a.shape)):
            raise ValueError(f"{self.name}: shape {a.shape}, expected {self.shape}")
        if not np.issubdtype(a.dtype, self.dtype):
            raise ValueError(f"{self.name}: dtype {a.dtype}, expected {self.dtype.__name__}")
        if np.isnan(a).any():
            raise ValueError(f"{self.name}: contains NaN")
        if a.min() < self.lo or a.max() > self.hi:
            raise ValueError(f"{self.name}: values in [{a.min():.3g}, {a.max():.3g}], expected [{self.lo}, {self.hi}]")
        return a

image_in = Contract("normalised image", (None, None), 0.0, 1.0)

good = np.random.default_rng(0).random((4, 4))        # already in [0, 1]
bad = (good * 255).round()                            # an upstream stage forgot to rescale

print("good input accepted:", image_in.check(good).shape)
try:
    image_in.check(bad)
except ValueError as e:
    print("bad input rejected :", e)
good input accepted: (4, 4)
bad input rejected : normalised image: values in [1, 238], expected [0.0, 1.0]

The second call fails with a message that names the interface and the violated condition. Without the contract, the model would run on values in \([0,255]\) and return an output with no error.

NoteWhere to place checks

Put a contract at every interface that crosses a team, a language, a process or a network. Inside one function, a test suite is enough.

45.7 Feedback to the designer

The chain in Equation 45.1 looks one-directional. A real project is a loop: requirements lead to a design, the design is characterised, the characterisation shows whether the requirement is met, and the result changes the design or the requirement. Chapter 46 treats the loops that exist inside the running system. Chapter 50 gives the protocol for the design loop.

45.8 Failure modes

Metric of the part, not of the whole. A component is optimised for its own score. The system score falls. Example: a detector tuned for recall floods the next stage with false alarms.

Silent contract breach. Units, colour order, normalisation and tensor layout differ between training code and serving code (Chapter 6).

Average-only reporting. The mean hides the hard condition where the system is not safe (Figure 45.2).

Test of parts without test of the whole. Every unit passes. The interfaces are wrong.

Test of the whole without test of parts. The system fails and nobody can say which stage to repair.

45.10 System connection

NoteWhat the system gained in this chapter

The system can now be described as a whole. It has components, interfaces with executable contracts, requirements that a test can decide, and a first model of how component errors combine: a product when failures are independent, and something different, and more structured, when they are not.

Components do not only feed forward. Outputs feed back into inputs, errors amplify along the chain, and a small change in one place can move the whole. Chapter 46 studies feedback, coupling and stability.

Chapter summary

  • A system is components, interfaces and a purpose. The model is one component.
  • A requirement names a condition, a measure, a level and a confidence.
  • With independent failures, system success is the product of stage success rates. With shared difficulty, the average rises and the worst case falls.
  • Contracts make interface errors loud.
  • Test the stages and the whole.

Exercises

  1. Compute. Five stages each have success rate 0.98. Give the system success rate if failures are independent. How many such stages reduce it below 0.9?
  2. Compute. A requirement asks for 99% success with 95% confidence. Name the four parts of the requirement and say which part is missing from “the system must reach 99%”.
  3. Derive. Show that for \(m\) independent stages with equal success rate \(p\), the system success rate falls below \(1/2\) when \(m>\ln 2/(-\ln p)\).
  4. Derive. In the shared-difficulty model, let hard inputs have fraction \(h\) and failure multiplier \(k_h\). Show that the easy multiplier is \(k_e=(1-hk_h)/(1-h)\) when the average failure rate is kept.
  5. Code. Change k_hard to 1.0, 2.0 and 3.0 in @code-series. Plot the system success on all inputs and on hard inputs.
  6. Code. Add a Contract for a probability vector (non-negative, sums to 1 within \(10^{-6}\)). Test it on a softmax output and on a vector of logits.
  7. Code. Build a three-stage pipeline of your choice and write the contract of each interface. Insert a unit error and show that the contract finds it.
  8. Diagnose. A document-question system scores 91% on retrieval alone and 94% on generation alone, using gold documents. The whole system scores 78%. Give two causes and an experiment for each.
  9. Explain. Why can the system be more reliable than the product of the stage rates, and why is this not good news?
  10. Design. Write a requirement for a face-unlock feature. Include operating condition, measure, level and confidence.
