21  Probability as a Language of Uncertainty

NoteKey ideas
  • A random variable is a number whose value is uncertain. A distribution says how likely each value is.
  • Expectation is the probability-weighted average. Variance measures the spread around it.
  • Covariance measures how two variables move together. Zero covariance does not mean independence.
  • The law of large numbers says that sample averages approach expectations. The central limit theorem says that their error is approximately Gaussian.
  • Some distributions have no mean or variance. Averages then do not settle.

21.1 The system problem

Everything so far treated data as exact. In the real world, it is not. Pixels have sensor noise. Labels are sometimes wrong. A model that reads “this is a 3” is usually more or less sure. Words in a sentence are uncertain until they are written.

How do we write down “I am not sure” so that it can be computed with?

Probability is that language. This chapter gives its vocabulary. Chapters 22–25 turn it into models and losses.

21.2 Random variables and distributions

A random variable \(X\) is a quantity whose value depends on chance. Its distribution assigns probabilities to values.

Discrete variables take countable values (a die, a class label). The probability mass function (PMF) is \(p(x)=P(X=x)\). It satisfies \(p(x)\ge0\) and \(\sum_xp(x)=1\).

Continuous variables take values on a line (a height, a pixel intensity). Their probability density function (PDF) \(p(x)\) gives probability by area: \[ P(a\le X\le b)=\int_a^bp(x)\,dx. \tag{21.1}\] A density can exceed 1. Only the area must be at most 1.

The cumulative distribution function (CDF) is \(F(x)=P(X\le x)\). It rises from 0 to 1 for both kinds.

from scipy.stats import norm
faces = np.arange(1, 7); pmf = np.array([0.1, 0.1, 0.1, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4])
fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 2, figsize=(9, 5))
axes[0, 0].bar(faces, pmf, color=PALETTE["data"]); axes[0, 0].set_title("PMF  p(x)")
axes[0, 1].step(np.r_[0, faces, 7], np.r_[0, np.cumsum(pmf), 1], where="post", color=PALETTE["model"])
axes[0, 1].set_title("CDF  F(x)")
xs = np.linspace(-4, 4, 400)
axes[1, 0].plot(xs, norm.pdf(xs), color=PALETTE["data"])
mask = (xs >= -1) & (xs <= 1)
axes[1, 0].fill_between(xs[mask], norm.pdf(xs[mask]), color=PALETTE["data"], alpha=0.35)
axes[1, 0].set_title("PDF  p(x)")
axes[1, 1].plot(xs, norm.cdf(xs), color=PALETTE["model"])
axes[1, 1].set_title("CDF  F(x)")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
print(f"P(-1 <= X <= 1) = F(1) - F(-1) = {norm.cdf(1) - norm.cdf(-1):.4f}")
P(-1 <= X <= 1) = F(1) - F(-1) = 0.6827

21.3 Expectation and variance

The expectation (mean) is the probability-weighted average:

\[ \mathbb{E}[f(X)]=\sum_xp(x)f(x)\quad\text{or}\quad\int p(x)f(x)\,dx. \tag{21.2}\]

It is linear: \(\mathbb{E}[aX+bY+c]=a\,\mathbb{E}[X]+b\,\mathbb{E}[Y]+c\), whether or not \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent.

The variance measures the spread around the mean \(\mu\):

\[ \text{Var}(X)=\mathbb{E}\bigl[(X-\mu)^{2}\bigr]=\mathbb{E}[X^2]-\mu^2, \qquad\text{std}(X)=\sqrt{\text{Var}(X)}. \tag{21.3}\]

TipWhy \(\text{Var}(X)=\mathbb{E}[X^2]-\mu^2\)

Expand the square and use linearity: \(\mathbb{E}[(X-\mu)^2]=\mathbb{E}[X^2-2\mu X+\mu^2]=\mathbb{E}[X^2]-2\mu\,\mathbb{E}[X]+\mu^2=\mathbb{E}[X^2]-\mu^2.\) \(\blacksquare\)

Two scaling rules follow: \(\text{Var}(aX+c)=a^2\,\text{Var}(X)\), and the standard deviation scales by \(|a|\).

21.4 Two variables: joint, marginal, covariance

For two variables, the joint distribution \(p(x,y)\) gives the probability of each pair. The marginal sums out the other variable:

\[ p(x)=\sum_yp(x,y). \tag{21.4}\]

\(X\) and \(Y\) are independent if \(p(x,y)=p(x)\,p(y)\) for all pairs.

The covariance measures linear co-movement:

\[ \text{Cov}(X,Y)=\mathbb{E}\bigl[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)\bigr]=\mathbb{E}[XY]-\mu_{X}\mu_Y. \tag{21.5}\]

The correlation \(\rho=\text{Cov}(X,Y)/(\sigma_{X}\sigma_Y)\) lies in \([-1,1]\) (by Cauchy–Schwarz, Chapter 4). The variance of a sum depends on it:

\[ \text{Var}(X+Y)=\text{Var}(X)+\text{Var}(Y)+2\,\text{Cov}(X,Y). \tag{21.6}\]

For independent variables \(\text{Cov}=0\) and variances add. This is the rule used in Chapter 20 for initialisation.

21.4.1 The covariance matrix

For a random vector \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d\), the covariance matrix has entries \(\Sigma_{jk}=\text{Cov}(x_j,x_k)\). The sample covariance of Chapter 7, \(\mathbf{S}=\frac1N\mathbf{X}_c^{\intercal}\mathbf{X}_c\), is the estimate of \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) from \(N\) samples. The eigenvectors of \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) are the axes of the distribution’s ellipse. This is why PCA works.

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
t = np.linspace(0, 2 * np.pi, 200); circ = np.stack([np.cos(t), np.sin(t)])
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(10.5, 3.4))
for ax, rho in zip(axes, [0.0, 0.8, -0.8]):
    C = np.array([[1.0, rho], [rho, 1.0]])
    Xs = rng.multivariate_normal([0, 0], C, size=800)
    S = np.cov(Xs.T, bias=True)
    lam, V = np.linalg.eigh(S)
    ax.scatter(*Xs.T, s=5, alpha=0.4, color=PALETTE["data"])
    ax.plot(*(V @ (2 * np.sqrt(lam)[:, None] * circ)), color=PALETTE["loss"], lw=2)
    ax.set_xlim(-4, 4); ax.set_ylim(-4, 4); ax.set_aspect("equal")
    ax.set_title(f"ρ = {rho:+.1f}   sample ρ̂ = {S[0, 1] / np.sqrt(S[0, 0] * S[1, 1]):+.2f}")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

21.4.2 Uncorrelated is not independent

Correlation sees only linear dependence. Take \(X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) and \(Y=X^2\). \(Y\) is a deterministic function of \(X\), so the two are strongly dependent. Yet their covariance is \(\mathbb{E}[X^3]-\mathbb{E}[X]\,\mathbb{E}[X^2]=0\).

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
x = rng.normal(size=200_000); y = x ** 2
print(f"corr(X, X²) = {np.corrcoef(x, y)[0, 1]:+.4f}   (≈ 0, yet Y is a function of X)")
big = np.abs(x) > 1.5
print(f"mean of Y when |X| > 1.5: {y[big].mean():.2f};  overall mean of Y: {y.mean():.2f}")
corr(X, X²) = -0.0054   (≈ 0, yet Y is a function of X)
mean of Y when |X| > 1.5: 3.92;  overall mean of Y: 1.00

21.5 Estimating from data: joint tables

We estimate a joint distribution of two binary pixels in the digits data (on if brightness \(>8\)) by counting.

from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
D = load_digits()
A = (D.data[:, 27] > 8).astype(int); B = (D.data[:, 28] > 8).astype(int)   # neighbouring pixels
joint = np.zeros((2, 2))
for a_, b_ in zip(A, B):
    joint[a_, b_] += 1
joint /= joint.sum()
pa, pb = joint.sum(axis=1), joint.sum(axis=0)             # marginals
print("joint p(a, b):\n", np.round(joint, 3))
print("marginals  p(a) =", np.round(pa, 3), "  p(b) =", np.round(pb, 3))
print("product    p(a) p(b):\n", np.round(np.outer(pa, pb), 3))
print(f"largest |joint − product| = {np.abs(joint - np.outer(pa, pb)).max():.3f}  -> not independent")
joint p(a, b):
 [[0.239 0.217]
 [0.118 0.426]]
marginals  p(a) = [0.456 0.544]   p(b) = [0.357 0.643]
product    p(a) p(b):
 [[0.163 0.293]
 [0.194 0.35 ]]
largest |joint − product| = 0.076  -> not independent

Neighbouring pixels are strongly dependent, as expected for images.

21.6 Law of large numbers

Let \(X_1,X_2,\dots\) be independent with the same mean \(\mu\). The sample mean \(\bar{X}_n=\frac1n\sum_{i\le n}X_i\) converges to \(\mu\) as \(n\) grows. This is the law of large numbers.

It makes Monte Carlo estimation work: to estimate \(\mathbb{E}[f(X)]\), average \(f\) over samples. The error shrinks like \(1/\sqrt{n}\).

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
rolls = rng.integers(1, 7, 20000)
running = np.cumsum(rolls) / np.arange(1, len(rolls) + 1)

truth = np.exp(0.5)                       # E[e^X] for X ~ N(0, 1)
ns = np.unique(np.logspace(1, 5, 20).astype(int))
errs = []
for n in ns:
    e = [abs(np.exp(rng.normal(size=n)).mean() - truth) for _ in range(100)]
    errs.append(np.mean(e))

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 3.3))
ax1.plot(running, color=PALETTE["data"]); ax1.axhline(3.5, ls="--", color=PALETTE["neutral"])
ax1.set_xscale("log"); ax1.set_xlabel("number of rolls"); ax1.set_ylabel("running mean")
ax2.loglog(ns, errs, "o-", color=PALETTE["model"], label="mean |error|")
ax2.loglog(ns, errs[0] * np.sqrt(ns[0] / ns), "--", color=PALETTE["neutral"], label="∝ 1/√n")
ax2.set_xlabel("samples n"); ax2.set_ylabel("error"); ax2.legend()
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

21.7 Central limit theorem

The sample mean not only converges. Its error is approximately Gaussian, whatever the original distribution (if the variance is finite):

\[ \bar{X}_n\;\approx\;\mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu,\ \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right). \tag{21.7}\]

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 4, figsize=(11, 2.8), sharey=False)
for ax, n in zip(axes, [1, 2, 5, 30]):
    means = rng.exponential(1.0, size=(20000, n)).mean(axis=1)
    ax.hist(means, bins=60, density=True, color=PALETTE["data"], alpha=0.6)
    xs = np.linspace(means.min(), np.quantile(means, 0.999), 300)
    ax.plot(xs, norm.pdf(xs, 1, 1 / np.sqrt(n)), color=PALETTE["loss"], lw=2)
    skew = np.mean(((means - means.mean()) / means.std()) ** 3)
    ax.set_title(f"n = {n}\nskewness {skew:.2f}", fontsize=10)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

The skewness falls as \(2/\sqrt{n}\). For \(n=30\), a Gaussian is a good description. This is why Gaussian noise is the default model of aggregated errors, and why batch averages behave well.

21.8 Heavy tails: when averages do not settle

The law of large numbers needs a finite mean. The Cauchy distribution has tails so heavy that its mean does not exist. Its sample mean never settles. It has the same distribution as one sample, however large \(n\) is.

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
n = 20000
g = rng.normal(size=n); c = rng.standard_cauchy(size=n)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.4, 3.3))
ax.plot(np.cumsum(g) / np.arange(1, n + 1), color=PALETTE["data"], label="Gaussian")
ax.plot(np.cumsum(c) / np.arange(1, n + 1), color=PALETTE["loss"], label="Cauchy")
ax.set_xscale("log"); ax.set_ylim(-5, 5); ax.legend()
ax.set_xlabel("number of samples"); ax.set_ylabel("running mean")
plt.show()

Real data, such as losses, gradient norms, or word frequencies, can be heavy-tailed. Check before trusting an average.

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

x = rng.normal(2.0, 1.5, size=100_000)
print("mean, var:", round(x.mean(), 3), round(x.var(), 3), " (truth 2.0, 2.25)")

      "  empirical:", round(np.mean(x <= 3.0), 4))

M = rng.multivariate_normal([0, 0], [[2.0, 1.2], [1.2, 1.0]], size=100_000)

21.10 Failure modes

Assuming independence. The variance of a mean is \(\sigma^2/n\) only for independent samples. Correlated samples (neighbouring pixels, consecutive frames, duplicates) give far less information than \(n\) independent ones.

Heavy tails. No finite mean or variance. Averages and standard deviations mislead. Use medians and quantiles.

Correlation read as causation or independence. Zero correlation does not mean independence (shown above). Nonzero correlation does not mean cause.

Too few samples. The central limit theorem is an approximation. For small \(n\) or skewed data, Gaussian error bars are wrong.

Estimating a covariance with too few samples. For \(N<d\), the sample covariance matrix is singular (rank at most \(N-1\)). Whitening (Chapter 13) then fails.

21.12 System connection

NoteWhat the system gained in this chapter

The system can now describe uncertainty: distributions, expectations, spread, and joint behaviour. It can estimate these from data and knows that averages converge at a \(1/\sqrt{n}\) rate, with Gaussian error, unless the tails are too heavy.

It cannot yet update beliefs with evidence, or turn a score into a probability. Chapter 22 adds conditioning and Bayes’ rule.

Chapter summary

  • A random variable has a distribution: PMF, PDF, CDF.
  • \(\mathbb{E}[X]\) is linear. \(\text{Var}(X)=\mathbb{E}[X^2]-\mu^2\).
  • Covariance measures linear co-movement. Independent implies uncorrelated, not the reverse.
  • \(\text{Var}(X+Y)=\text{Var}(X)+\text{Var}(Y)+2\,\text{Cov}(X,Y)\).
  • Sample means converge (LLN) with Gaussian error \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\) (CLT).
  • Heavy tails break both: the Cauchy mean never settles.
