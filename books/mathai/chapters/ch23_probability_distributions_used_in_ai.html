23  Probability Distributions Used in AI

NoteKey ideas
  • A handful of distributions cover most of AI: Bernoulli, categorical, Gaussian, and their relatives.
  • The Gaussian is closed under linear maps: if \(\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\), then \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}^{\intercal})\).
  • The reparameterisation trick writes a random sample as a deterministic function of parameters and fixed noise, so gradients can flow through it.
  • The KL divergence between two Gaussians has a closed form.
  • Heavy tails, collapsed variances, and discrete/continuous confusion are the standard traps.

23.1 The system problem

Chapters 21 and 22 gave the general language. A model must commit to specific distributions. A classifier outputs a distribution over classes. A regression model with noise assumes a distribution over errors. A diffusion model adds noise of a known shape.

Which few distributions describe nearly every model output, noise source, and prior, and what can we compute with them?

23.2 Discrete distributions

Name Describes PMF Mean Variance
Bernoulli\((p)\) one yes/no trial \(p^x(1-p)^{1-x}\) \(p\) \(p(1-p)\)
Binomial\((n,p)\) successes in \(n\) trials \(\binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}\) \(np\) \(np(1-p)\)
Categorical\((\boldsymbol{\pi})\) one of \(K\) classes \(\pi_k\) — —
Multinomial\((n,\boldsymbol{\pi})\) counts over \(K\) classes \(\frac{n!}{\prod k_j!}\prod\pi_j^{k_j}\) \(n\pi_k\) \(n\pi_k(1-\pi_k)\)

The categorical distribution is the output of every classifier and every language model: a vector \(\boldsymbol{\pi}\) of \(K\) non-negative numbers that sum to one (the softmax of Chapter 24 produces it). Sampling from it is simple: draw \(u\sim\text{Uniform}(0,1)\) and pick the first class whose cumulative probability exceeds \(u\).

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
pi = np.array([0.5, 0.3, 0.15, 0.05])
cum = np.cumsum(pi)

def sample_categorical(pi_cum, n):
    u = rng.random(n)
    return np.searchsorted(pi_cum, u)             # first index with cum >= u

draws = sample_categorical(cum, 100_000)
print("target   :", pi)
print("empirical:", np.round(np.bincount(draws, minlength=4) / len(draws), 3))
target   : [0.5  0.3  0.15 0.05]
empirical: [0.501 0.301 0.148 0.05 ]

Language-model decoding (Chapter 33) is this operation, repeated: compute a categorical distribution over the vocabulary, then sample.

23.3 The Gaussian

The Gaussian (normal) distribution has density

\[ \mathcal{N}(x;\mu,\sigma^2)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right). \tag{23.1}\]

Its mean is \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\). About 68%, 95%, and 99.7% of the mass lie within \(1\sigma\), \(2\sigma\), \(3\sigma\) of the mean.

Three facts make the Gaussian the workhorse.

  1. Central limit theorem (Chapter 21): sums of many small independent effects are approximately Gaussian.
  2. Closure: a sum of independent Gaussians is Gaussian, with means and variances added. Any affine map of a Gaussian is Gaussian.
  3. Maximum entropy: among all distributions with a given mean and variance, the Gaussian has the largest entropy. It assumes the least beyond those two numbers.
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
a = rng.normal(1.0, 2.0, 500_000)
b = rng.normal(-3.0, 1.5, 500_000)
s = a + b
print(f"sum: mean {s.mean():.3f} (expect {1.0 - 3.0}),  var {s.var():.3f} (expect {4.0 + 2.25})")
within = [np.mean(np.abs(a - 1.0) < k * 2.0) for k in (1, 2, 3)]
print("fraction within 1, 2, 3 sigma:", np.round(within, 4))
sum: mean -1.996 (expect -2.0),  var 6.254 (expect 6.25)
fraction within 1, 2, 3 sigma: [0.6817 0.9545 0.9972]

23.3.1 The multivariate Gaussian

For \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d\) with mean \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) and covariance \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) (positive definite),

\[ \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|^{1/2}}\exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac12(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\Bigr). \tag{23.2}\]

The exponent is half the squared Mahalanobis distance of Chapter 13. The contours are ellipses with axes along the eigenvectors of \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\).

TipDerivation: mean and covariance of a linear map

Let \(\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}\). By linearity of expectation, \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}]=\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\mu}+\mathbf{b}\). The deviation is \(\mathbf{y}-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}]=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\). Then \[ \text{Cov}(\mathbf{y})=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^{\intercal}\mathbf{A}^{\intercal}\bigr]=\mathbf{A}\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\mathbf{A}^{\intercal}. \] For a Gaussian \(\mathbf{x}\), \(\mathbf{y}\) is also Gaussian, so these two numbers describe it fully. \(\blacksquare\)

This gives the standard way to sample: take \(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})\), find a matrix \(\mathbf{L}\) with \(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{\intercal}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) (the Cholesky factor), and set \(\mathbf{x}=\boldsymbol{\mu}+\mathbf{L}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\). Then \(\text{Cov}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{I}\mathbf{L}^{\intercal}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\).

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
A = np.array([[2.0, 0.5], [0.0, 0.8]]); b = np.array([3.0, 1.0])
eps = rng.normal(size=(2000, 2))
y = eps @ A.T + b
S_pred = A @ A.T
S_emp = np.cov(y.T, bias=True)
print("predicted covariance A Aᵀ:\n", np.round(S_pred, 2))
print("sample covariance:\n", np.round(S_emp, 2))

L = np.linalg.cholesky(S_pred)
y2 = rng.normal(size=(200_000, 2)) @ L.T + b                  # Cholesky sampling
print("Cholesky sampling covariance:\n", np.round(np.cov(y2.T), 2))

23.4 Tails: Gaussian, Laplace, Student-\(t\)

Other distributions differ mainly in how fast the tails fall.

Distribution Density (up to scale) Tails Appears in
Gaussian \(e^{-x^2/2\sigma^2}\) very light squared-error loss; noise
Laplace \(e^{-|x|/b}\) exponential absolute-error loss; L1 prior (Chapter 25)
Student-\(t\) \((1+x^2/\nu)^{-(\nu+1)/2}\) polynomial (heavy) robust models; Cauchy at \(\nu=1\) 
from scipy import stats
xs = np.linspace(-8, 8, 400)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.2, 3.4))
ax.semilogy(xs, stats.norm.pdf(xs, 0, 1), color=PALETTE["data"], lw=2, label="Gaussian")
ax.semilogy(xs, stats.laplace.pdf(xs, 0, 1 / np.sqrt(2)), color=PALETTE["grad"], lw=2, label="Laplace")
ax.semilogy(xs, stats.t.pdf(xs, 3, scale=0.6), color=PALETTE["loss"], lw=2, label="Student-t (ν=3)")
ax.set_ylim(1e-16, 1); ax.set_xlabel("x"); ax.set_ylabel("density"); ax.legend()
plt.show()
print(f"density at x = 6:  Gaussian {stats.norm.pdf(6):.1e},  "
      f"Laplace {stats.laplace.pdf(6, 0, 1/np.sqrt(2)):.1e},  Student-t {stats.t.pdf(6, 3, scale=0.6):.1e}")
density at x = 6:  Gaussian 6.1e-09,  Laplace 1.5e-04,  Student-t 5.2e-04

A model with Gaussian noise punishes an outlier by its square (Chapter 14). A model with Laplace noise punishes it linearly. This is the origin of the MSE and MAE losses (Chapter 25).

23.5 Changing variables

If \(Y=g(X)\) for a monotone, differentiable \(g\), the density of \(Y\) is

\[ p_Y(y)=p_{X}\bigl(g^{-1}(y)\bigr)\,\Bigl|\frac{d\,g^{-1}(y)}{dy}\Bigr|. \tag{23.3}\]

The factor \(|dg^{-1}/dy|\) accounts for how \(g\) stretches the axis. In several dimensions it is \(|\det\mathbf{J}_{g^{-1}}|\) (the Jacobian of Chapter 17). This formula is the basis of normalising flows.

Example: if \(X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) and \(Y=e^X\), then \(g^{-1}(y)=\ln y\) and \(p_Y(y)=\frac{1}{y}\mathcal{N}(\ln y;0,1)\), the log-normal density.

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
y = np.exp(rng.normal(size=400_000))
lo, hi = 0.5, 2.0
emp = np.mean((y > lo) & (y < hi))
from scipy.integrate import quad
formula = quad(lambda t: stats.norm.pdf(np.log(t)) / t, lo, hi)[0]
print(f"P({lo} < Y < {hi}): simulated {emp:.4f}, from the change-of-variables density {formula:.4f}")
P(0.5 < Y < 2.0): simulated 0.5106, from the change-of-variables density 0.5118

23.6 The reparameterisation trick

Models often need a gradient of an expectation with respect to a distribution’s parameters:

\[ \nabla_\mu\,\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)}\bigl[f(z)\bigr]. \]

The sample \(z\) is random, so it is not obvious how to differentiate through it. The reparameterisation trick writes the sample as a deterministic function of the parameters and parameter-free noise:

\[ z=\mu+\sigma\,\epsilon,\qquad\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1). \tag{23.4}\]

Now \(\mathbb{E}[f(z)]=\mathbb{E}_\epsilon[f(\mu+\sigma\epsilon)]\), and the gradient moves inside the expectation: \(\nabla_\mu\mathbb{E}[f]=\mathbb{E}_\epsilon[f'(\mu+\sigma\epsilon)]\). This is a sample average of ordinary derivatives. It is used in VAEs (Chapter 40) and in the diffusion training objective (Chapter 41).

There is another way: the score-function (REINFORCE) estimator, \(\mathbb{E}[f(z)\,\nabla_\mu\ln p(z)]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[f(z)\frac{z-\mu}{\sigma^2}\bigr]\). It works for any distribution but has a far higher variance. We compare them for \(f(z)=z^2\). The true gradient is \(\nabla_\mu\mathbb{E}[z^2]=\nabla_\mu(\mu^2+\sigma^2)=2\mu\).

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
mu, sigma, n, reps = 1.0, 1.0, 100, 3000
eps = rng.normal(size=(reps, n))
z = mu + sigma * eps
reparam = (2 * z).mean(axis=1)                          # mean of f'(z) = 2z
score = (z ** 2 * (z - mu) / sigma ** 2).mean(axis=1)    # mean of f(z) (z - mu)/sigma^2
print(f"true gradient: {2 * mu}")
print(f"reparameterised: mean {reparam.mean():.3f}, std {reparam.std():.3f}")
print(f"score function : mean {score.mean():.3f}, std {score.std():.3f}")
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6, 3.3))
ax.hist(score, bins=80, alpha=0.6, color=PALETTE["loss"], label="score function", density=True)
ax.hist(reparam, bins=80, alpha=0.7, color=PALETTE["grad"], label="reparameterised", density=True)
ax.axvline(2, color="black", ls="--"); ax.set_xlabel("estimate"); ax.legend()
plt.show()
true gradient: 2.0
reparameterised: mean 2.001, std 0.204
score function : mean 2.000, std 0.552

23.7 KL divergence between Gaussians

Chapter 15 defined \(D_{\text{KL}}\). For two univariate Gaussians it has a closed form. For the case that matters in VAEs, a Gaussian against the standard normal:

\[ D_{\text{KL}}\bigl(\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)\,\Vert\,\mathcal{N}(0,1)\bigr)=\tfrac12\bigl(\mu^2+\sigma^2-1-\ln\sigma^{2}\bigr). \tag{23.5}\]

TipDerivation

\(D_{\text{KL}}=\mathbb{E}_p[\ln p(z)-\ln q(z)]\) with \(\ln p(z)=-\tfrac12\ln(2\pi\sigma^2)-\frac{(z-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\) and \(\ln q(z)=-\tfrac12\ln2\pi-\tfrac12z^2\). Under \(p\), \(\mathbb{E}[(z-\mu)^2]=\sigma^2\) and \(\mathbb{E}[z^2]=\mu^2+\sigma^2\). So \[ D_{\text{KL}}=-\tfrac12\ln\sigma^2-\tfrac12+\tfrac12(\mu^2+\sigma^2)=\tfrac12(\mu^2+\sigma^2-1-\ln\sigma^2).\ \blacksquare \] For a diagonal multivariate Gaussian, sum this over the coordinates.

mu, sigma = 0.8, 0.6
closed = 0.5 * (mu ** 2 + sigma ** 2 - 1 - np.log(sigma ** 2))
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
z = rng.normal(mu, sigma, 2_000_000)
mc = np.mean(stats.norm.logpdf(z, mu, sigma) - stats.norm.logpdf(z, 0, 1))
print(f"closed form {closed:.5f}   Monte Carlo {mc:.5f}")
closed form 0.51083   Monte Carlo 0.51137

23.8 Production

torch.distributions bundles these with sampling and log-densities. rsample is the reparameterised sample (gradients flow). sample is not (gradients stop).

import torch
from torch.distributions import Normal, Categorical, MultivariateNormal, kl_divergence

mu_t = torch.tensor(0.8, requires_grad=True)
sd_t = torch.tensor(0.6, requires_grad=True)
d = Normal(mu_t, sd_t)

z = d.rsample((50_000,))                       # reparameterised: z = mu + sd * eps
(z ** 2).mean().backward()
print(f"rsample gradient d/dmu E[z²]: {mu_t.grad.item():.3f}  (truth 2·mu = {2 * 0.8})")
print("sample() keeps gradients:", d.sample((3,)).requires_grad)

print("closed-form KL matches torch:",
      np.isclose(kl_divergence(Normal(0.8, 0.6), Normal(0.0, 1.0)).item(), closed))
print("Categorical log_prob:", Categorical(torch.tensor(pi)).log_prob(torch.tensor(2)).item(),
      "  ln(0.15) =", round(float(np.log(0.15)), 4))
mvn = MultivariateNormal(torch.tensor(b), covariance_matrix=torch.tensor(S_pred))
print("MVN log_prob at mean:", round(mvn.log_prob(torch.tensor(b)).item(), 4),
      "  formula:", round(float(-np.log(2 * np.pi) - 0.5 * np.log(np.linalg.det(S_pred))), 4))

23.9 Failure modes

Density is not probability. A density can exceed 1. Only its integral is a probability. Comparing a density to a probability is a type error.

Variance collapse. A maximum-likelihood Gaussian fitted to one point has likelihood growing without limit as \(\sigma\to0\). Models that learn their own variance need a floor or a prior.

x0, mu0 = 0.7, 0.7                                    # one data point, mean placed on it
for s in [1.0, 0.1, 0.01, 0.001]:
    print(f"sigma = {s:6.3f}   log-likelihood = {stats.norm.logpdf(x0, mu0, s):7.2f}")
sigma =  1.000   log-likelihood =   -0.92
sigma =  0.100   log-likelihood =    1.38
sigma =  0.010   log-likelihood =    3.69
sigma =  0.001   log-likelihood =    5.99

Wrong tails. A Gaussian noise model assigned to heavy-tailed data lets rare large errors dominate the fit. Use a Laplace or Student-\(t\) model, or a robust loss (Chapter 14).

Underflow. Densities of many variables are tiny products. Use log_prob always. Compare log-densities, and avoid exponentiating.

Not positive definite. A sampled or learned covariance may fail to be positive definite. Parameterise through a Cholesky factor or a diagonal.

Discrete versus continuous. The pixel intensity is an integer in 0–255. A continuous model assigns it a density. Dequantisation (adding small noise) is needed to compare likelihoods fairly.

23.11 System connection

NoteWhat the system gained in this chapter

The system has a vocabulary of distributions: the discrete ones for classes, the Gaussian for continuous noise and latents, and tools to transform them and sample from them. It can differentiate through random samples (reparameterisation) and measure the distance between Gaussians in closed form.

Models do not output distributions directly. They output scores. Chapter 24 shows how scores become probabilities.

Chapter summary

  • Bernoulli, binomial, categorical, multinomial are the discrete workhorses. The categorical is the output of classifiers and language models.
  • The Gaussian is closed under affine maps: \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\mu}+\mathbf{b},\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}^{\intercal})\).
  • Sample a Gaussian as \(\boldsymbol{\mu}+\mathbf{L}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\) with \(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{\intercal}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\).
  • Laplace and Student-\(t\) have heavier tails and correspond to robust losses.
  • Change of variables multiplies the density by \(|dg^{-1}/dy|\).
  • Reparameterisation \(z=\mu+\sigma\epsilon\) gives low-variance gradients through samples.
  • \(D_{\text{KL}}(\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)\Vert\mathcal{N}(0,1))=\frac12(\mu^2+\sigma^2-1-\ln\sigma^2)\).

  3. Derive. Show that the sum of two independent Gaussians has variance \(\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2\) (use Equation 21.6 of Chapter 21).
  4. Derive. Show that if \(z=\mu+\sigma\epsilon\) then \(\partial z/\partial\sigma=\epsilon\). Use it to write the reparameterised gradient of \(\mathbb{E}[f(z)]\) with respect to \(\sigma\).
  5. Derive. The KL divergence between \(\mathcal{N}(\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)\) and \(\mathcal{N}(\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)\) is \(\ln\frac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1}+\frac{\sigma_1^2+(\mu_1-\mu_2)^2}{2\sigma_2^2}-\frac12\). Verify it numerically.
  6. Code. Fit a Gaussian and a Student-\(t\) by maximum likelihood to data with 2% outliers. Compare the estimated means.
  7. Code. Generate samples from the Dirichlet distribution with \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(1,1,1)\) and \((10,10,10)\). Plot them on a triangle. What does \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\) control?
