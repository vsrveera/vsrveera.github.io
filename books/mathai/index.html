Preface

Do not organize AI around mathematics. Organize mathematics around the needs of AI.

Why this book exists

Many people use AI systems every day. Fewer can say what happens inside one. The explanation usually comes in one of two forms. The first is a list of mathematical subjects: linear algebra, then calculus, then probability, then optimisation, each with its own examples. The second is a list of model architectures, each with a diagram and little else. The first form does not show why a subject matters. The second form does not show where the numbers come from.

This book takes a different route. It starts from what an AI system must do, and it brings in the mathematics when the system needs it.

A system that learns from data must do six things:

  1. Represent information as numbers.
  2. Transform those numbers into something more useful.
  3. Measure how wrong the result is.
  4. Learn by changing its parameters to reduce the error.
  5. Reason under uncertainty, because data are noisy and incomplete.
  6. Generalise to inputs it has not seen.

Each task has a mathematical language. Vectors and geometry represent. Matrices and functions transform. Distances and information measure. Calculus and optimisation learn. Probability handles uncertainty. Statistics and learning theory say what generalisation means. The six languages are not six courses. They are six parts of one machine.

The test I used while writing is simple. When you see

\[ \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf Q\mathbf K^{\intercal}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)\mathbf V, \]

you must be able to say what every symbol is, why every operation is there, and what would go wrong without it. Chapter 30 does this for attention. Chapter 44 does it for a whole system.

What is different in this book

Every chapter begins with a system problem. Not with a definition. A chapter asks a question that a working system cannot avoid, such as “how does a machine decide how much to trust each part of its input?” The mathematics follows as the answer.

Every chapter ends with what the system gained. A recurring map of the system (World, Data, Representation, Transformation, Measurement, Learning, Uncertainty, Generalisation, AI system) highlights where we are. At the end of a chapter, a short note says which new capability the system has and which capability it still lacks. That lack is the question of the next chapter.

All code is executed, and the numbers in the text come from the output. Each chapter contains short Python programs. Most use only NumPy. They run when the book is built, and the figures and numbers in the text come from that run. When a claim has a number, you can find the line of code that produced it. Random seeds are fixed. If your numbers differ in the last digits, the cause is the library version or the machine, not an error in the text.

Results that do not match the simple story are reported. Several experiments in this book give a result that a simple argument does not predict. A ranking of two models flips under noise (Chapter 51). A first-order error estimate is too optimistic when two lines are nearly parallel (Chapter 48). A tuned threshold fails to meet its requirement on fresh data (Chapter 50). I kept these results and explained them. They are the points where the mathematics teaches more than a clean example does.

Systems thinking is not a decoration. Part IX is a full part of the book, not an appendix. A model is one component of a system. Components have interfaces. Interfaces break. Errors accumulate and feed back. A system claim without a measured condition and a confidence interval is an opinion. The last seven chapters give the method to check such claims. They follow the programme of performance characterisation that R. M. Haralick, V. Ramesh and their co-workers developed for computer vision: state the input and its perturbation, define the algorithm and its parameters, describe the output distribution, and validate against real data. The examples in this book are my own small constructions in that spirit. Please check the original papers (listed in Chapter 47) before you cite them.

Who should read it

The book is written for engineers, students and researchers who use machine-learning tools and want to understand what is under them.

You need You do not need
High-school algebra and the idea of a function A course in linear algebra, calculus or probability
Basic Python: functions, loops, lists Prior machine learning
Willingness to run code and change it A GPU

Calculus, linear algebra and probability are taught inside the book, at the point of need, with the same system example in view. If you already know them, the chapters still show how they connect to training and inference. You can skim the first half of each such chapter and start at the system problem.

How the book is organised

Part Chapters Central question
I. The Mathematical Machine 1–2 What is an AI system, mathematically, and how do we write it down?
II. Representation 3–7 How do we turn the world into numbers that keep what matters?
III. Transformation 8–12 How do we compute useful new representations?
IV. Measurement 13–15 How do we say how far apart, how wrong, how informative?
V. Learning 16–20 How do we change the parameters to make the error smaller?
VI. Uncertainty 21–25 How do we reason when data are noisy and incomplete?
VII. Generalisation 26–29 Why should a model work on data it has not seen?
VIII. Putting the Mathematical Systems Together 30–44 How do attention, Transformers, vision and generative models use all of this?
IX. Systems Thinking and Performance Characterisation 45–51 How do we know that a system works, under which conditions, and with what confidence?

Parts I to VII build the toolkit. Part VIII uses it on the systems that dominate current practice. Chapter 44 shows that one small model and one unfamiliar architecture can be taken apart with the same tools. Part IX turns from “how does it work” to “how well, and how do we know”.

The shape of a chapter

Most chapters have the same parts, so that you can find things.

Section Purpose
Key ideas The five points to take away. Read first.
The system problem The question the system cannot avoid.
Mathematics and code Definitions, derivations, and a program that checks them.
Failure modes How the idea goes wrong in practice.
Where this appears in modern AI A table that links the chapter to current systems.
System connection What the system gained, and what is missing.
Summary and exercises Ten exercises: compute, derive, code, diagnose, explain, design.

Three ways to read it

  • From the start. The chapters depend on earlier chapters and never on later ones. A forward reference is a pointer, not a requirement.
  • Toward Transformers. Read Parts I and II, Chapters 8 to 11, 13 to 14, 16 to 18, 21 to 24, and then Chapters 30 to 32.
  • Toward evaluation. Read Chapters 1, 14, 16 to 18, 21 to 27, and then Part IX. Part IX uses probability, the idea of generalisation, and the Jacobian from the chain rule.

Conventions

  • Notation. Scalars are italic (\(a\), \(\lambda\)). Vectors are bold lowercase (\(\mathbf x\)). Matrices are bold uppercase (\(\mathbf W\)). Tensors are calligraphic (\(\mathcal X\)). The transpose is \(\intercal\). Chapter 2 gives the full table.
  • Column and row. In mathematics, vectors are columns. In code, a batch of examples is a matrix with one row per example. So the mathematical \(\mathbf W\mathbf x\) becomes X @ W.T in code. Each chapter shows the transpose where it matters.
  • Language. I write short sentences with one idea each and use one word for one meaning. I follow the ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English style for explanation. Definitions, theorems and derivations keep the usual mathematical form. The style is for readers whose first language is not English, and for all readers who want to find the point quickly.
  • Spelling. British: generalisation, characterisation, optimiser.
  • Honest scope. Experiments are small on purpose, so that they run on a laptop and every number can be checked. They show the mechanism, not the scale. Where scale changes the behaviour, the chapter says so.

What is not here

This book is not a survey of every architecture, nor a recipe book for training a large model on a cluster. It does not cover reinforcement learning in depth, graph networks, or the engineering of distributed training. It treats large models through the mathematics they share with small ones, and it stays close to the quantities you can compute and check. The exercises at the end of each chapter are the place to go further.

A note on use

Run the code. Change a number and see what moves. Break an equation on purpose and look at the error. The exercises marked Code are the ones that most change how well the ideas stay with you. Where a chapter says “check this”, it means that you should.
